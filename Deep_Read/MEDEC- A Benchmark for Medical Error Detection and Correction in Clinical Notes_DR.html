MEDEC
Can a model check a note it didn't write?

Full paper: "MEDEC: A Benchmark for Medical Error Detection and Correction in Clinical Notes" · Ben Abacha, Yim, Fu, Sun, Yetisgen, Xia, Lin (Microsoft Health & Life Sciences AI · University of Washington) · ACL Findings 2025

Almost every medical benchmark asks a model to produce an answer. This one asks it to validate text someone else wrote — find the single clinical error planted in a note, say which sentence it's in, and fix it. Two medical doctors did the same task on the same notes, which turns the leaderboard into something more interesting than a ranking.

The task

Three subtasks, deliberately separated

In plain termsRead a clinical note. Is anything in it medically wrong? If so, which sentence — and what should it say instead? Three questions, scored separately, because a model can get any one right while failing the others.

Subtask A · Detect

Is there an error?

A binary flag: 0 if the note is clean, 1 if it contains an error. Scored by accuracy — and note that 48.7% of test notes are clean, so a model that flags everything scores badly.

Subtask B · Locate

Which sentence?

Return the sentence ID containing the error, or −1 for a clean note. Every note is presented one sentence per line, each prefixed with its ID.

Subtask C · Correct

What should it say?

Generate the corrected sentence. Scored with ROUGE-1, BERTScore, BLEURT (averaged into an Aggregate Score) plus MIST, a metric built on UMLS medical-concept embeddings rather than word overlap.

Each note contains at most one error, drawn from five types selected by analyzing the most frequent question types in official medical board exams: Diagnosis (174 test cases), Management (168), Treatment (58), Pharmacotherapy (57), and Causal Organism (18).

How the errors got there

Two injection methods, on purpose

Errors are planted, not naturally occurring — which is the only way to know the ground truth. The paper uses two very different planting methods so the benchmark isn't shaped by a single approach's quirks.

Method MS · from board exams

Swap the right answer for a plausible wrong one

Starting from MedQA board-exam vignettes, four medically-trained annotators first verified the original question–answer pairs and discarded any that were ambiguous or missing context. They then wrote the answer into the narrative — the correct answer to make a clean note, a plausible wrong answer to make an erroneous one — in two variants, with the error placed mid-text or at the end.

2,189 training / 574 validation / 597 test texts. Publicly available.

Method UW · from real notes

Replace a real concept with a near neighbour

488 real diagnosis-support notes (2009–2021) from Harborview, UW Medical Center, and Seattle Cancer Care Alliance. Four medical students picked a medical entity — candidates surfaced as UMLS concepts — and swapped it for a similar-but-distinct concept, suggested either by the annotator or by a SNOMED/LLM-based method.

244 of the 488 had errors injected. De-identified after injection, independently reviewed by two annotators, with a third adjudicating discrepancies. Requires a data use agreement.

One design constraint in the UW method is worth pulling out: each injected error span was required to contradict at least two other parts of the same note, and annotators had to write a justification for every error introduced. That makes the UW subset a test of internal consistency checking specifically — the error isn't detectable from outside medical knowledge alone; it's detectable because the note now disagrees with itself.

The finding that complicates the leaderboard

Being best at finding errors does not mean being best at fixing them

Detection and correction rank almost inversely across the evaluated models. This is the paper's sharpest result, and it means "which model should we use" has no single answer.

Claude 3.5 Sonnet is first at detecting errors and last at correcting them. DeepSeek-R1 is near the bottom at detection (51.68%) yet second at correction. The paper's own explanation for Claude is partly stylistic — it writes long corrections with explanatory prose, which lexical metrics punish. Notably, on MIST (the UMLS-concept metric, which compares medical concepts rather than words) Claude scores highest of all models at 0.9325 — so part of that "last place" is the metric, not the medicine.

The human baseline, and what it revealed

Doctors won — but agreed with each other only 69% of the time

Two medical doctors did the same three subtasks on 569 of the 925 test notes, with 242 double-annotated to measure agreement.

Humans still ahead on detection

Doctor #1: 79.61% flag accuracy · 65.88% sentence.
Doctor #2: 71.61% flag · 66.77% sentence.
Best model (Claude): 70.16% flag · 65.62% sentence.

On correction, Doctor #2 also leads everything at 0.7118 aggregate — but Doctor #1 scores only 0.4527, below several models. The two doctors differ from each other more than the top models differ from each other.

The agreement number is the real finding

Inter-annotator agreement between the two doctors: 69.01% on whether an error exists, 57.85% on which sentence contains it.

Two qualified physicians, given the same note, disagree about whether it contains a medical error roughly a third of the time. That sets a hard ceiling on what any "accuracy against a single reference" number can mean here — and it makes the task's difficulty a property of the clinical judgment involved, not just of the models.

Two more results worth carrying

Over-flagging, and a public/private performance gap

Recall is high, accuracy is not — so precision is the problem

Models invent errors in clean notes

o1-preview's recall on notes that genuinely contain an error is very high across every type: Diagnosis 96.6%, Management 93.5%, Treatment 93.1%, Pharmacotherapy 96.5%, Causal Organism 100%. Yet its overall accuracy (67.46%) sits below the doctors'.

The paper reads this directly: the model "hallucinated error presence in many cases" — it finds nearly every real error, but also flags errors in notes that are fine. Given that 48.7% of test notes are clean, that failure mode is expensive.

MS (public) vs UW (private) split

A gap that looks like familiarity

o1-preview on the MS subset (built from publicly available board-exam material): 72.9% flag / 68.8% sentence. On the UW subset (real private notes, never seen by any LLM): 57.6% / 47.9%.

Roughly a 15- and 21-point drop moving from public-derived to private text. The doctors show no comparable pattern — Doctor #2 actually scores higher on UW (77.2% vs 68.9%). A benchmark drawn only from public sources would have reported the optimistic number.

Where the scoring struggles

The synonym problem, shown by the paper's own example

Case ms-test-234: a boy with recurrent bacterial infections and two maternal uncles who died similarly; the note wrongly says common variable immunodeficiency.

Reference correction

"Patient is diagnosed with Bruton agammaglobulinemia…"

What GPT-4, GPT-4o, o1-preview, o1-mini, Claude and Doctor #2 all said

"Patient is diagnosed with X-linked agammaglobulinemia…"

These are the same disease. Every lexical metric penalizes the mismatch anyway — which is precisely why the authors include MIST, which compares UMLS concept embeddings rather than surface strings, and why the aggregate ranking and the MIST ranking disagree. The general lesson: when the reference is one clinician's phrasing, a metric that scores words will mistake vocabulary for error.

Reading it with its scope attached

What the benchmark does and doesn't establish

Errors are injected, not naturally occurring

Exactly one planted error per note, of five predefined types. Real documentation errors are messier, can co-occur, and include categories outside this taxonomy — the authors list dataset size and error-type coverage as explicit limitations.

The MS reference reflects the exam's answer key

The authors note plainly that MS items "reflect the original content and answer choices as provided in those sources" and that "medical professionals may hold differing opinions" — the 69% inter-doctor agreement is the same fact seen from the other side.

Prompting was deliberately basic

Two prompts only — zero-shot and one-shot. The authors report that in-context learning and chain-of-thought helped older models but not newer ones, and explicitly leave retrieval-augmented and instruction-based prompting to future work.

Open-domain models only

Specialized medical models weren't evaluated — Med-PaLM in particular was not publicly available. So the paper doesn't tell us whether domain fine-tuning helps or hurts on validation tasks specifically.
