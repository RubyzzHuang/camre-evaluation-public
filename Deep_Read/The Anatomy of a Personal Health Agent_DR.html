The Anatomy of a Personal Health Agent
An evaluation framework built one capability at a time

Full paper: "The Anatomy of a Personal Health Agent" · Heydari, Gu, Srinivas, Yu et al. (Google Research · Google DeepMind)

This paper builds a consumer health agent out of three specialists (data science, medical knowledge, coaching) plus an orchestrator — but the more transferable contribution is how it evaluates that system: every architectural component is decomposed into named capabilities, and every capability gets its own purpose-built benchmark, before the pieces are ever assembled and re-tested as a whole.

Where the architecture comes from

Four user needs, mined before a single agent was built

The sub-agent boundaries were not chosen a priori. They were derived from analyzing real web-search and health-forum queries plus a user-centered design process with end-users and health experts, which converged on four critical user journeys (CUJs):

CUJ1 · General health & wellness knowledge

Open factual questions about medicine, fitness, and wellness — not tied to the asker's own data.

CUJ2 · Personal data insights

"What is my average resting heart rate on days my sleep score is above 86?" — reading and interpreting one's own wearable/lab data.

CUJ3 · Wellness advice

"To improve my sleep score, what time should I go to bed?" — actionable, often goal-oriented guidance.

CUJ4 · Personal medical symptoms

"My throat is itchy and I have trouble swallowing — what might be going on?" — often needs back-and-forth to clarify.

The three sub-agents map onto these needs directly: the Data Science (DS) Agent answers CUJ2, the Domain Expert (DE) Agent answers CUJ1 and CUJ4, and the Health Coach (HC) Agent answers CUJ3 — with an Orchestrator handling queries (very common in practice) that need more than one at once.

The paper's real discipline is this: nothing gets a system-level claim until each piece has been benchmarked on its own, against its own purpose-built test, with its own human raters. Every method and figure below is transcribed from the paper — nothing invented.

A recurring discipline

Before trusting an automated grader, it had to earn it

Human expert time is the scarcest resource in this whole framework (1,120+ hours), so wherever an LLM autorater is used to scale up grading, it is first checked against the same experts it's meant to replace — not assumed reliable.

The DS Agent's plan-quality autorater

Built from 141 expert-rated query→plan pairs (10 data scientists, 47 hours, 6,157 rubric-item annotations). A Gemini 2.0 Flash autorater trained on part of that set was checked against a held-out 96-case test set: ICC(3,1) = 0.838 agreement with the human experts — high enough to trust for scaling the comparison.

The human raters agreed with each other too

Before the autorater was even built, the 10 expert data scientists' own ratings were checked for consistency: 80.2% average agreement, Bennett's S = 0.622 — a moderate-to-good inter-rater reliability baseline the autorater then had to be measured against, not an assumed ceiling.

Evaluation feeding back into design

A measured weakness, fixed before the next evaluation

The Health Coach (HC) Agent was evaluated on six expert-derived capabilities (HC1–HC6: goal identification, active listening, context clarification, user empowerment, SMART recommendations, feedback incorporation). It won 5 of 6 head-to-head comparisons against a baseline coach — but lost, clearly and consistently, on one:

End-users noticed it too

In live, 1.5-hour sessions with 31 real participants (48 human-hours), the HC Agent lost specifically on "progress measurement": 48.7±3.9% vs. the baseline's 61.3±4.5% (p=0.010) — the one theme where a simpler agent that "covers everything" had an edge over the more structured one.

So did the coaching experts

Three of the same experts who defined HC1–HC6 re-rated the transcripts, blind to condition, and found the identical gap on progress-measurement (46.7% vs. 50.3%, p<0.001) — converging evidence from two independent rater pools.

The paper is explicit that this finding was acted on directly: the Health Coach module was rebalanced to give more weight to progress-tracking before it was wired into the full multi-agent system evaluated next — a visible instance of a benchmark result becoming an architecture change, not just a footnote.

Assembling the pieces

Two baselines, chosen to isolate what the orchestrator actually adds

Rather than only comparing the full system to "no system," the evaluation isolates which design choice matters by testing two deliberately weaker multi-agent baselines side by side with the same three specialist agents and the same base model:

Baseline A

Single agent (PHIA)

One monolithic agent tries to do data science, domain expertise, and coaching itself. No specialization at all.

Baseline B

Parallel multi-agent

All three specialists answer the same query independently and simultaneously; an orchestrator just stitches the three answers together. Specialized, but static — no task assignment, no reflection, no memory.

Full system

PHA (this paper's design)

Orchestrator infers intent → assigns a main agent + supporting agents → each response gets a self-critique reflection pass → a persistent memory carries goals and context forward across turns.

This 3-way design directly tests the paper's own stated principles for why a naive multi-agent setup isn't enough: Comprehensive Needs, Adaptive Support, Low User Burden, and Simplicity — the Parallel baseline satisfies Comprehensive Needs and Simplicity but not Adaptive Support or Low User Burden; the Single-agent baseline satisfies none of the first three.

An important precision on realism: the 150 conversations rated here were generated by an LLM (Gemini 2.0 Flash) role-playing 50 real WEAR-ME participant personas — not live humans typing in real time. That's a different, weaker standard of realism than the HC Agent's own Task 1 above (31 real people, live, 1.5-hour sessions) or the DE Agent's diagnostic-reasoning benchmark (2,000 real self-reported cases) — worth keeping distinct when reading the numbers below.
Who's rating matters

End-users and experts agreed on the winner — and disagreed on everything else

20 end-users, 150 scripts

The full system (PHA) was preferred 48.7% of the time vs. 24.8% (parallel) and 26.5% (single-agent) — roughly double either baseline (p<0.001). Its biggest edge was in Personal-Data-Analysis and Data-Results-Interpretation, exactly where the DS and DE agents' outputs need to be synthesized rather than just concatenated.

5 health/coaching experts, same 150 scripts

The gap was far starker: PHA rated 84.8% overall quality vs. 70.8% (parallel) and 65.2% (single-agent), and picked as best in 80.0% of cases vs. 15.6% and 4.4% (~270 expert-hours, 750 ratings + 250 rankings).

The sharpest disagreement was between the two baselines, not the winner: end-users rated the single-agent and parallel-agent baselines about the same, but experts ranked the single-agent system worst almost three times as often as end-users did — a professional-competence gap that was largely invisible to lay raters but obvious to people trained to spot it.

What the paper is candid about

Reading the numbers with their own caveats attached

The DS Agent still misses distributions

Its analysis plans frequently overlooked data-distribution considerations — a gap the authors attribute to the difficulty of inferring distributions from limited personal data, not something the architecture fixed.

Code generation is slow, not just imperfect

Even at a 79.0% eventual pass rate, generation-execution-correction cycles took 20–50 seconds per query, often to resolve multi-table join errors — a latency cost the paper reports plainly rather than smoothing over.

System-level realism is one step removed

As noted above, the 150-conversation PHA evaluation used LLM-simulated personas rather than live users — real for the sub-agent-level benchmarks, simulated at the point where the pieces are assembled and compared as a whole.

Everything is validated against one cohort

The real-world grounding data (WEAR-ME, N=1,165) is a single wearable-and-blood-biochemistry study; the 50 personas used across multiple tasks are drawn from just 10 common health profiles within it.
