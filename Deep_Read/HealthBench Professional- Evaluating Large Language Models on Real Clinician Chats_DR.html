HealthBench Professional
The sequel built from real clinician chats, not crowdsourced tasks

Full paper: "HealthBench Professional: Evaluating Large Language Models on Real Clinician Chats" · Soskin Hicks, Trofimov, Lim, Arora, Singhal et al. (OpenAI)

Same rubric-scoring engine as the original HealthBench, but a completely different sourcing philosophy: every example is a real conversation a physician had with ChatGPT for Clinicians while doing actual clinical work — not a scripted vignette — then aggressively filtered down to the cases current frontier models still get wrong.

What's actually different from HealthBench

Real usage logs, not authored scenarios — plus a harder filter

Source

Live product conversations

Every example is a conversation a physician actually had with ChatGPT for Clinicians during its development — good-faith daily use, or deliberate red-teaming — rather than a physician sitting down to author a synthetic scenario.

Construction

3-stage authorship, not 1

Stage 1: a physician writes the rubric and a 1–7 Likert rating of the model's response. Stage 2: an independent physician checks rubric quality and realism. Stage 3: final adjudication resolves ambiguity before release.

Selection

Deliberately hunts for failures

Stratified sampling by Likert difficulty enriches "difficult" (1–2) examples by roughly 3.5× overall — 8× within good-faith examples alone — to keep the benchmark from saturating against current frontier models.

A trustworthiness safeguard tied to that last point: for every example flagged as "difficult" for OpenAI's own frontier models, two additional physicians had to independently confirm the model actually failed — the point being that a low score should reflect a real model limitation, not evaluation noise or an unclear rubric.

Scoring — same engine, two upgrades

The rubric formula is unchanged; the grader and the length handling aren't

Each example still carries physician-written rubric criteria worth between −10 and +10 points; a response earns a criterion's full value if met, zero otherwise, and the example score is the ratio of earned points to the maximum possible:

score = ( Σ points of met criteria ) / ( Σ points of positive criteria ), clipped to [0, 1]

A stronger default grader

The rubric grader is GPT-5.4 at low reasoning — "a broadly more capable model than the GPT-4.1 grader used in HealthBench." The paper leans on the original HealthBench's meta-evaluation (model–physician agreement ≈ physician–physician agreement) rather than re-running that validation here.

An empirically-fit length penalty

Longer answers mechanically satisfy more rubric criteria. The authors ran multiple models across verbosity settings, fit a linear slope of score against response length (in the 0–4,000 character range, to stay in the locally-linear regime), and got ≈1.47 points per 500 characters above a 2,000-character baseline — subtracted from every score before reporting.

The adjustment visibly works: raising verbosity from low to medium added ~3.9 points to the unadjusted score across models, but was nearly flat after length-adjustment — confirming the raw gain was mostly length, not quality. Reasoning effort, by contrast, kept improving scores even after adjustment (+3.3 points on average, up to +5.6–7.3 for GPT-5.4) — genuine test-time-compute quality gains, not just longer output.

The human comparison point

An intentionally strong physician baseline

Who

A specialty- or subspecialty-matched physician wrote each response — someone whose training lines up with the case.

Conditions

Unbounded time and internet access (guidelines, literature, drug databases) — but no AI assistance. Framed explicitly as an upper bound on achievable human quality, not an average clinician under real time pressure.

Bias control

Physicians never wrote the reference response for an example they had originally authored, to prevent self-evaluation bias.

The model isn't the whole system

Most of the gain comes from the product harness — but not everywhere

Three conditions share the same base model (GPT-5.4): the bare model, the model with only a browsing tool bolted on, and the full ChatGPT for Clinicians harness (browsing plus evidence retrieval across millions of peer-reviewed studies, plus other product scaffolding).

Writing & documentation: the harness matters most

64.1 (full harness) vs. 34.6 (base GPT-5.4, p=3.4×10⁻¹⁰) vs. 19.7 (browsing only, p=3.5×10⁻¹⁴). The product scaffolding — not just web access — is doing most of the work here.

Care consult: harness advantage disappears

51.0 (full harness) vs. 42.7 (physicians, p=0.025) — a real edge over humans, but statistically indistinguishable from base GPT-5.4 (p=0.72) or browsing-only (p=0.32). For this use case, the underlying model's reasoning is doing the work, not the harness.

What the paper is upfront about

The absolute score is not a real-world performance rate

The benchmark is deliberately unrepresentative

Because difficult examples were enriched ~3.5×, the authors state plainly: "a moderate aggregate score (e.g., 45%) can coexist with high real-world performance in typical usage." Most real clinician interactions fall in the easier range the benchmark deliberately downweights.

A partial self-referential confound

GPT-5.4 was the model used for the majority of red-teaming and difficulty labeling in the first place — which the authors acknowledge "partially confounds comparisons across models," even while noting OpenAI models still perform well despite this selection pressure.

Scope is also explicitly bounded to three chat use cases (care consult, writing/documentation, medical research) — EHR-integrated workflows and institution-specific constraints are out of scope, and the authors recommend institutions run their own pre- and post-deployment evaluations rather than relying on this benchmark alone.
