Does it actually help — in a real clinic?

Full paper: “AI-based Clinical Decision Support for Primary Care: A Real-World Study” · Korom, Kiptinness et al. (Penda Health) · Arora, Singhal et al. (OpenAI) · 2025

Every other paper here evaluated models offline. Why this paper matters

Offline capability ≠ real-world impact

A model can ace HealthBench and still fail in a clinic if clinicians don't trust it, don't see the alert in time, or it doesn't fit the workflow. Penda found three factors all had to be present.

Factor 1

Model performance

A capable prompted model (GPT-4o — chosen for low latency over raw capability, so feedback is timely enough to act on).

Factor 2

Clinically-aligned implementation

Many iterations to fit the EMR workflow, local epidemiology, and Kenyan guidelines — surfacing alerts only at decision points, minimizing interruption.

Factor 3

Active deployment

Strategies to build clinician understanding and buy-in. The effect grew after an induction period drove uptake.

The intervention

A traffic-light safety net

AI Consult runs asynchronously in the background at each decision node (history, investigations, diagnosis, treatment) and surfaces guidance only when needed — the clinician keeps full control.

For each decision node the model returns a colour + rationale + suggested action. The front-end maps the colour to escalating intrusiveness:

Green

Just a green checkmark — no interruption at all.

Yellow

A ringing bell the clinician can choose to open — advisory, non-blocking.

Red

A pop-up the clinician must view & acknowledge — safety-critical (but they still keep the final call).

The prompt gives the model Penda-specific context + a guideline summary + category definitions + few-shot red/yellow/green examples. Model-agnostic by design — any model can be swapped in; Penda chose GPT-4o for low latency (fast feedback matters more for adoption than raw capability).

Explore — the headline result

Fewer errors at every stage of the visit

Clinicians were cluster-assigned — half the clinicians in each clinic got AI Consult (AI group), half didn't (non-AI). Independent physicians rated each de-identified visit on a 5-point Likert per stage (History · Investigations · Diagnosis · Treatment); Likert 1–2 = a clinically meaningful error (see the full scale below). Each bar is the relative risk reduction in that error rate, AI vs non-AI (Table 3), cluster- and covariate-adjusted; NNT = visits per one averted error; "yearly errors averted" projects to Penda's ~400,000 annual visits. How they measured "agreement" — and the twist

Two measures, each computed for physician↔physician (baseline) and model↔physician:

Within 1 Likert point

On the 5-pt scale, "agree" if scores are within ±1 — lenient ordinal closeness.

Fleiss' κ on error vs no-error

Collapse to error (Likert 1–2) vs not (3–5), then chance-correct (0 = chance, higher = better; ~0.2–0.3 = "fair").

The twist: model↔physician agreement actually exceeded physician↔physician agreement — yet the LLMs still reported larger effect sizes. Agreeing with any single rater is not the same as being unbiased on the aggregate number.

The evaluation machinery

What made the error-rating trustworthy

The rating pipeline is the transferable gold here — a checklist of the discipline behind the numbers.

Golden reference examples
Consensus of 3 physician investigators set a worked example for each Likert value, shared with the rater panel to anchor scoring.
Rater training
Video onboarding, "office hours", ongoing clarification — reducing subjectivity across the panel.
Double-rating + agreement
~25% of visits (1,387 / 5,666) rated by two physicians; agreement within one Likert point, and error-agreement vs chance via Fleiss' κ.
Blinded to arm & identity
Records stripped of patient and clinician identifiers before rating.
Clinically interpretable stats
Relative risk reduction + NNT; Fisher's exact test; Katz-method CIs; covariate- and cluster-adjusted (Poisson/GEE). Plain: report the effect in units clinicians read instantly, and account for patients clustering within the same clinician/clinic (they aren't independent).
Multiple-comparison control
Benjamini-Hochberg across the four clinical domains to control false-discovery rate. Plain: because four things were tested at once, tighten the bar so a lucky fluke doesn't get counted as a real effect.

Safety-report classification — in detail

Where the 12 reports came from: Penda's existing Patient Safety Reporting (PSR) system (running since 2020) — any staff member can raise a quality/safety concern, most often the customer-service team when a patient experiences harm or potential harm. It captures both unavoidable harm (e.g. a new drug allergy) and near-misses (serious errors that didn't cause harm). Over the 10-week study there were 12 PSRs: 5 in the non-AI group, 7 in the AI group.

Each report was independently reviewed on three axes:

1 · Quality lapse?

Was there a clinical quality lapse at all?

2 · Severity

How serious was the event?

3 · AI Consult's role

Did it cause harm, fail to prevent it, or could have prevented it?

What the review found — on axis 3:

0
caused harm / created new risk
3

~½
of reports: harm could have been prevented if AI Consult were used and its guidance followed

Careful — that "~half could have been prevented" mixes two different situations:
(a) Control group had no tool. Some were non-AI-group visits where AI Consult would have raised a red, but those clinicians simply didn't have it — so "not prevented" reflects absence of the tool, not a tool failure.
(b) AI group didn't follow / didn't see the alert. Other cases were AI-group visits where a critical alert was ignored, or wasn't reliably visible (one occurred early in the induction period before red alerts displayed dependably). This is an adherence & visibility gap, not a reasoning gap.
Both deaths reviewed were judged potentially preventable with correct AI Consult use — underscoring that the bottleneck is often whether guidance is seen and heeded, not just whether the model is right.

Second-order effects

The tool also upskilled the clinicians

Learning effect

Red alerts fell 45% → 35%

Over the study, AI-group clinicians increasingly avoided "red" mistakes before being flagged — the safety net taught better practice, not just caught errors. An outcome worth measuring in its own right.

Adoption & trust

100% positive, 75% “substantial”

All surveyed AI-group clinicians said it improved their care. Adoption (thumbs-up rate, visit duration, doc length) was tracked as a first-class outcome — because an unused tool has zero clinical benefit.
