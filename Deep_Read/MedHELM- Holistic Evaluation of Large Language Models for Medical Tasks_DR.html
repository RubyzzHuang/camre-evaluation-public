MedHELM
From exam scores to real clinical work

Full paper: “MedHELM: Holistic Evaluation of Large Language Models for Medical Tasks” · Bedi, Cui, Fuentes, Unell et al. (Stanford), Shah et al. · arXiv:2505.23802 (May 2025)

LLMs already ace medical licensing exams — but that says little about the messy day-to-day work of medicine. MedHELM answers a structural question HealthBench doesn't: what is the full landscape of medical tasks, and how do we measure a model across all of it? Its answer — a clinician-validated task taxonomy — is the skeleton CAMRE's evaluation plan can be organized around.

Why it exists

Exam accuracy is not clinical competence

MedHELM identifies three structural gaps in how medical LLMs were being evaluated — and positions itself as complementary to HealthBench, not a replacement.

Gap 1

Synthetic, narrow tasks

Benchmarks lean on synthetic vignettes or exam questions, missing real diagnostic work like extracting the relevant details from a patient record.

Gap 2

Almost no real EHR data

Only ~5% of medical LLM evaluations use real EHR data — which carries the ambiguity, inconsistency, and shorthand synthetic data can't reproduce.

Gap 3

Task monoculture

~64% of evaluations focus only on licensing exams and diagnosis — ignoring documentation, patient messaging, and administrative work that fill a clinician's day.

MedHELM vs HealthBench — complementary lenses

HealthBench

5,000 single-turn, direct-to-patient dialogues; model acts autonomously. Physician rubrics reward exhaustive, risk-averse answers — a stress test for a fully autonomous chatbot. But synthetic, no task taxonomy, no co-pilot / structured-workflow tasks.

MedHELM

A taxonomy-guided suite across real clinician workflows — an assistive co-pilot doing note generation, order review, literature summarization, billing. Uses real EHR data and cost-aware metrics.

Contribution 1 · The skeleton

A clinician-validated task taxonomy

Five categories → 22 subcategories → 121 discrete tasks, built with 29 clinicians across 14 specialties. Click a category to expand its subcategories. The bar shows how well current models do there — note where they struggle.

Validation

96.7% category-assignment agreement

Clinicians independently re-assigned each subcategory to a top-level category and matched the intended one 96.7% of the time — evidence the structure reflects how clinicians actually conceptualize their work. Comprehensiveness rated 4.21 / 5.

Two design rules

Medical relevance + clear boundaries

Every task maps to something providers routinely do; categories are drawn to minimize overlap while keeping meaningful functional distinctions. This is what makes the taxonomy usable as a reporting scaffold.

Contribution 2 · Full coverage

35 benchmarks, one per corner of the taxonomy

A taxonomy is only useful if every cell is actually measured. MedHELM built a suite covering all 22 subcategories with a three-tiered curation strategy — and standardized how any dataset becomes a benchmark.

17 existing

Adopt

Public/gated benchmarks that already fit a subcategory (MedQA, MIMIC-IV billing, ACI-Bench).

5 reformulated

Transform

Un-evaluated medical datasets turned into benchmarks by adding a standardized prompt template + metric.

13 new

Create

Private Stanford Healthcare datasets for under-served Administration & Workflow tasks (referral triage, scheduling).

Every benchmark item has the same anatomy

Context

Raw input — a clinical note, patient message, or structured EHR record.

Prompt

Standardized instruction template (“answer in 2–3 sentences”, MCQ framing…).

Metric

Matched to task type — see below.

Gold response (optional)

Reference output; framework also supports benchmarks without one.

Metric matched to task type — exact-match accuracy for single-token/numeric answers · micro-F1 for multi-label tasks (e.g. ICD-10 coding) · LLM-jury for open-ended text generation. One benchmark suite, several scoring regimes.

Contribution 3 · Try it — the flagship method

Scoring open-ended answers with an LLM-Jury

A single LLM judge is noisy and biased. MedHELM instead convenes a 3-member jury (GPT-4o, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, LLaMA 3.3 70B — deliberately different providers), each scoring on a 1–5 Likert scale across 3 axes. The final score is the mean of all 9 ratings. Switch responses and watch the grid — then see how a single judge would have misled us.

The exact rubric each judge is given copy this for CAMRE

Two levels of definition. The top line is the conceptual axis; the highlighted line is the verbatim question the judge model actually answers (from the ACI-Bench jury prompt, Appendix F). Each score is on a 1–5 scale (1 = very poor, 5 = excellent).

Axis 1

Accuracy

Factual correctness and adherence to medical guidelines.

What the judge is asked (1–5)“Does the note provide correct clinical information based on the conversation?”
Axis 2

Completeness

Thoroughness in addressing all aspects of the query.

What the judge is asked (1–5)“Does the note include all important medical details from the conversation?”
Axis 3

Clarity

Organization, readability, easy-to-understand language.

What the judge is asked (1–5)“Is the note written clearly and organized in a standard clinical format for clinicians?”

Four operational details that make this usable

  • Justification is mandatory. Each judge must return strict JSON — {"accuracy":{"score":…,"explanation":"…"}, …} — so every number is auditable, not a black box.
  • Gold is used "when needed". The prompt says compare to the reference note only when needed — which is why low-quality gold answers didn't shift model rankings.
  • Axes are swappable per task. For NoteExtract (reformat with no gold), Completeness → Structure (did it reorganize into the requested format?).
  • Final score = mean of 9 (3 judges × 3 axes), decoded at temperature 0 for determinism.

What the numbers said

Where models are strong — and where they aren't

Reasoning models led (DeepSeek R1 66% win-rate, o3-mini 64%), but Claude 3.5 Sonnet matched ~63% at ~40% lower cost. More useful than the ranking is the per-category gradient: the taxonomy reveals a consistent weak spot.

Performance gradient by category

The cost-performance lesson

Reasoning models cost most (DeepSeek R1 ~$1,806, o3-mini ~$1,722 per full run) for the top win-rates; Claude 3.5 Sonnet (~$1,571) sat on the efficient frontier. One full leaderboard update ≈ $11,739.

Takeaway: Administration & Workflow is the universal weak spot (0.53–0.63). The taxonomy didn't just rank models — it localized where they fail, which a single aggregate score would have hidden.
