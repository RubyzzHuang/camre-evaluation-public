LongHealth
Knowing what's in a chart is not the same as knowing what's missing

Full paper: "LongHealth: A Question Answering Benchmark with Long Clinical Documents" · Adams, Busch, Han, Excoffier, Kather, Truhn, Bressem et al.

Ten models are ranked three different ways in this benchmark — extract from one patient's chart, extract with other patients' charts mixed in as noise, and answer when the actual patient's chart isn't there at all. The leaderboard barely resembles itself across these three tests, and the third one exposes a near-total blind spot: the best score, across every model tested, is 29%.

Two separate axes — easy to conflate

What's being asked, vs. what's in front of the model

LongHealth crosses two independent things: the kind of question, and the context condition the model has to answer it under. Worth keeping apart, since the flagship below only varies the second one.

Content — Extraction

Single-hop retrieval: the answer is stated once, somewhere in the documents. Find it.

Content — Negation

"What treatment was not given?" — requires piecing together everything that was mentioned, across possibly several documents, to correctly conclude an absence.

Content — Sorting

Put events (e.g. diagnostic tests) in the order they actually happened, even though the source documents may present them out of chronological order.

Separately, each question is also asked under one of three evaluation tasks — this is the axis the flagship explores:

Task 1

Single patient, shuffled

Just the target patient's own documents, reordered five times to control for position effects.

Task 2

Target patient + distractors

The target patient's documents mixed in with other patients' unrelated documents — same question, noisier context.

Task 3

Target patient absent entirely

Only other patients' documents are shown — the model is asked about someone whose chart was never provided, with an explicit "cannot be answered" option added.

The best model at finding answers is not the best model at admitting it can't

Same 10 models, same questions — only the evaluation task changes. Pick a task to see how the ranking shifts.

Mixtral-8x7B leads Task 1 (77%) and Task 2 (71%) by a wide margin — then collapses to 17% on Task 3, behind five other models. The model that actually wins Task 3, Mistral-7B-Instruct, tops out at just 29%. One model (longchat-13b-16k) answers zero of its Task 3 questions correctly. General retrieval capability and recognizing the limits of one's own knowledge are evidently not the same skill, and don't scale together.

A second, quieter finding

More context isn't free, even within a model's stated limit

Task 1 was re-run at input lengths from 4,000 to 32,000 tokens (via padding with more distractor text) to isolate the effect of length alone, holding the actual question and answer constant.

Most models degrade with length

GPT-3.5-Turbo dropped from 72% (4k tokens) to 68% (16k). Vicuna-7B and Zephyr-7B dropped 13 points each. This happened well within each model's advertised context window — the limit being reached wasn't a hard token cutoff, it was retrieval reliability degrading gradually as the haystack grew.

A few long-context-native models held steady

Yi-34B and Mixtral-8x7B showed only minor fluctuation out to 32k tokens — architecture and pretraining for long context mattered more than context-window size on the spec sheet.

Reading the "sorting" task precisely

Chronological ordering is not the same as staleness-awareness

Worth being precise about what the Sorting category actually measures, since it's the closest thing in this paper to broader "longitudinal reasoning."

What Sorting does test

Given several dated events scattered non-sequentially across documents, can the model reconstruct the correct chronological order? This is a multi-hop extraction-and-ordering task.

What it does not test

Whether a model, when synthesizing a current clinical judgment, correctly discounts an old value as potentially stale rather than treating it as still valid — especially when no more recent value exists at all. That's a distinct downstream-reasoning question this benchmark doesn't probe; it stops at whether facts can be extracted and ordered, not whether their age is correctly weighted into a final synthesized answer.

What the paper is upfront about

Reading the numbers with their own caveats

Prompting wasn't optimized

The authors explicitly flag that Task 3 in particular "may have yielded better results with alternative prompting techniques" — the same fixed prompt was used everywhere for fair comparison, at the cost of possibly understating what's achievable.

GPT-4 was priced out

Early tests showed GPT-4 outperforming the field, but evaluating it at 16,000-token context across all questions was estimated at $2,200 for Task 1 alone — excluded on cost grounds, not because it wasn't tried.

Multiple-choice, not open-ended

Real clinical queries rarely come with a fixed answer set. MCQ format enables automated scoring but doesn't capture the full difficulty of an open-ended clinical question.

Fictional cases, no sub-16k models

All 20 cases are synthetic (built from realistic but fictional discharge summaries), and models with context windows under 16,000 tokens were excluded entirely — their performance under retrieval-augmented or sliding-window strategies remains untested here.
