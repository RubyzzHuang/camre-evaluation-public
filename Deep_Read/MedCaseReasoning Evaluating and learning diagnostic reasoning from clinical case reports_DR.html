MedCaseReasoning
Right diagnosis, wrong reasons?

Full paper: “MedCaseReasoning: Evaluating and learning diagnostic reasoning from clinical case reports” · Kevin Wu, Eric Wu, Rahul Thapa et al. (Stanford · USC · UCSF) · arXiv:2505.11733

A prior study found frontier models can land on the correct diagnosis for the wrong reasons in up to a third of cases. MedCaseReasoning is built to catch exactly that: 14,489 diagnostic cases, each paired with the clinician's own enumerated reasoning extracted from a real case report — so accuracy and reasoning quality are scored separately. It's also released as a training set: fine-tuning on it measurably improves both.

Why it exists

Accuracy alone hides a reasoning problem

Exam-style benchmarks like MedQA and MMLU only score the final answer. Medical diagnosis, unlike math or code, needs the process to be right too — a model can hit the right label while skipping or fabricating the clinical logic that should justify it.

The gap

Right answer, wrong reasons

Prior work found even GPT-4 reaches the correct diagnosis for incorrect reasons in up to a third of scenarios — invisible to accuracy-only benchmarks.

The source

Real case reports, not vignettes

Built from PubMed Central case reports across 800+ journals — not textbook-style questions, and far more diverse than a single-hospital series.

The scale

14K+ vs. a few hundred

Comparable efforts (e.g. NEJM CPC-based sets) run in the hundreds of cases. MedCaseReasoning is ~50× larger and fully open-access.

How the dataset is built

Case report → structured QA, with two quality gates

Every case report is split into three parts by an LLM: the case presentation (patient info), the diagnostic reasoning (the case author's own enumerated reasoning steps — including ruled-out alternatives), and the final diagnosis. Two filters then weed out weak conversions.

Gate 1 — five clinician-written criteria, scored by o4-mini

Thoroughness of case presentation (1–5) · dependence on integrative clinical reasoning (yes/no) · discusses 2+ plausible alternatives · transparency of the diagnostic reasoning (1–5) · states a final diagnosis (yes/no). Cases scoring 1–2 on thoroughness, or missing alternatives/a stated diagnosis, are dropped.

Human check on the pipeline output: 4 board-certified physicians reviewed 100 finished cases (~10–20 min each). 98% free of hallucination in the case prompt/reasoning · 92% final diagnoses faithful & inferable from the case · 93% of reasoning steps faithful and clinically relevant.

The exact metric

Definition 1: Reasoning Recall

For case i, let Rᵢ = the clinician's ground-truth reasons (an LLM-extracted enumerated list from the case report) and Tᵢ = the reasons found in the model's own trace.

cᵢ = |Rᵢ ∩ Tᵢ| ÷ |Rᵢ|   →   Reasoning Recall = mean(cᵢ) over N cases

How the overlap is judged

Fully automated: o4-mini as an LLM-judge returns a JSON decision for each ground-truth reason — found in the trace, or not. Validated against a board-certified physician's annotations.

Two practical caveats

Recall is scored on the best-of-10 trace (a randomly selected correct response, or an incorrect one if none were correct). It's not computable for OpenAI o3 and similar frontier models — their full internal reasoning isn't exposed via API, only accuracy is.

Note this measures recall, not precision — whether the model's trace covers the clinician's points, not whether the model added anything extraneous. And crucially, the ground-truth reasons here include ruled-out alternatives (e.g. "Wilson-disease recurrence — ruled out because…"), not just the supporting evidence for the final answer — so it's checking whether the model walked the same differential-elimination path, not just cited the right facts.

It's also a training set

Fine-tuning on these traces measurably helps

The extracted reasoning is a list of quoted points, not a fluent trace — so an LLM "stitches" the list into a coherent narrative before it's used for supervised fine-tuning (SFT), without adding new information.

A bias-control trick worth noting

Stitching with a stronger model risks injecting that model's own style/priors into the training data. The fix: the model being fine-tuned is the same model that does its own stitching — no cross-model contamination of reasoning style.

Does it generalize?

Cross-checked against the closed NEJM CPC set

As external validation, the same models were also run on 302 NEJM Clinicopathologic Conference cases (gated, not open-access, not used in training). Model rankings and accuracy levels on MedCaseReasoning's open test set correlated strongly with performance on this independent, closed benchmark — evidence that MedCaseReasoning is a valid open proxy for harder-to-access gold-standard case collections.
