TIMER
Where in the timeline a question points is itself a variable

Full paper: "TIMER: Temporal Instruction Modeling and Evaluation for Longitudinal Clinical Records" · Cui, Unell, Chen, Fries, Alsentzer, Koyejo, Shah (Stanford) · ICML 2025

Every benchmark built on longitudinal records implicitly decides which part of the patient's history its questions are about — and until this paper, nobody was controlling that. TIMER makes temporal position an explicit, measurable axis, shows that both human-written and model-written questions cluster badly, and then demonstrates that fixing the clustering in training data measurably improves temporal reasoning.

What "TIMER" actually refers to

One framework, two deliverables

Easy to conflate, and they answer different questions. This deep-read is mostly about the first, since that's the evaluation contribution — but the second is what makes the paper's argument land.

TIMER-Bench

An evaluation set with temporal position controlled

402 instruction–response pairs over real longitudinal records, each carrying explicit time evidence (the dated events its answer rests on). Because every question is tagged with where in the timeline it points, the benchmark can be re-sampled into different temporal distributions on demand.

TIMER-Instruct

A tuning method that balances temporal coverage

Instruction tuning on 5,000 model-generated pairs whose temporal positions are deliberately distributed rather than left to chance. Same records, same volume — only the distribution over the timeline changes.

What a TIMER-Bench question actually looks like

The paper's own showcase example

Worth seeing before the failure cases below, because it shows the benchmark at its most demanding — a question whose answer only exists if five separate periods are assembled into one causal narrative.

Instruction

"Explain how the patient's response to lung cancer treatment changed after discovering the EGFR mutation, and why they ultimately returned to the initial therapy."

01/2020Initial lung cancer diagnosis; chemotherapy started, good response
05/2020Genetic testing reveals EGFR mutation → switched to targeted therapy
08/2020–06/2021Notes show exceptional response, tumor shrinkage
09/2021Resistance develops, new growth
01/2022–12/2022Original chemotherapy restarted, excellent control

Answering the "why did they end up back where they started" half requires connecting the 09/2021 resistance to the 01/2022 switch-back, and understanding that the return isn't a reversal of a mistake but a response to a changed situation. No single visit contains that; it exists only across the span.

Questions were filtered to keep only those carrying multiple time-stamped pieces of evidence. Worth noting what that filter does and doesn't guarantee: it enforces that an answer draws on more than one point in time, but two timestamps alone don't make a question this demanding — a question asking for a height at age 9 and again at age 11 also passes it. The paper's clinician check reflects this: complexity was rated 80/100 on average but with wide spread across the three raters (77 / 63 / 99), against much tighter agreement on clinical relevance (97 / 89 / 99).

The problem being named

Existing benchmarks quietly only ask about recent history

Two failure shapes, in the two most widely used sources of EHR instruction data:

Limitation 1 · MIMIC-derived instruction data

Not longitudinal at all

MIMIC-Instr pairs are generated from single ICU visits averaging 7.2 days. 84.5% of its patients are single-visit. Whatever it measures, it cannot be multi-visit temporal reasoning — the data simply doesn't span it.

Limitation 2 · MedAlign (clinician-written)

Long records, but the questions bunch at the end

MedAlign covers an average span of 3,895 days (~10.7 years) — genuinely longitudinal records. But the clinician-authored questions asked about them concentrate heavily at the recent end:

55.3% of all 396 instructions
Last 15%
47.0%

29.5% — nearly a third of all questions land in the last twentieth of the record

The paper is fair about why: reviewing a decade of history to write a question is cognitively expensive, and recent summaries genuinely do carry trajectory information. But it means a model can score well while never being asked to reason about the middle of a patient's history. Compounding it, 71.3% of MedAlign questions are retrieval-based — needle-in-a-haystack lookups rather than synthesis across the timeline.

The enabling measurement

Normalized temporal position

None of the above is measurable without a way to compare timelines of wildly different absolute lengths. The paper's device is simple and is what makes the rest of the work possible: rescale every piece of time evidence to where it sits between the first and last visit in the context window.

Pj = (Tj − Tmin) ⁄ (Tmax − Tmin)

A value of 0 means the evidence sits at the very start of the record, 1 at the very end. Clinical events arrive irregularly — clustered during acute episodes, sparse during stable periods — so raw dates can't be compared across patients. This normalization makes "where in the history does this question point" a single number that is comparable across records, which is what turns temporal coverage into something that can be plotted, audited, and deliberately controlled.

A second bias, this one from the machine

Left to itself, an LLM writes questions about the beginning and the end

Since human curation of long records is expensive, the obvious move is to have an LLM generate the instruction data. The paper checks what that produces — and finds the generator has its own temporal bias, in a different shape from the human one.

"Lost in the middle." Plotting the normalized position of every piece of time evidence in model-generated instruction–response pairs shows heavy density at both ends of the timeline and a pronounced trough through the middle periods. Where clinicians over-weight the recent end, the LLM over-weights both ends — and both of them skip the middle.

This matters beyond benchmark construction: if instruction-tuning data inherits this shape, the tuned model learns to attend where its training questions pointed, and the middle of a patient's history stays a blind spot in the trained model too.

Try it — does training distribution have to match evaluation distribution?

Three tuning sets, identical except for where in the timeline their questions point

Same patient records, same number of examples — only the temporal distribution differs. Each tuned model is then scored on three benchmarks with three different distributions. Pick a training distribution to see how it does across all three. All numbers are the paper's own (Table 4), LLM-judged.

The headline is that alignment mostly helps — but the exception is the interesting part. On the edge-focused benchmark, the uniformly-trained model actually scores highest on correctness (65.17 vs. the aligned edge model's 64.68). Balanced temporal coverage in training turns out to be competitive even on a benchmark it isn't matched to, while the recency-trained model is the weakest whenever the benchmark asks about anything other than recent history.

What "bad temporal reasoning" concretely looks like

Three named failure modes, with real before-and-after cases

The paper's case study compares the base model against the same model after temporal instruction tuning. The value here is less the improvement than the vocabulary — three distinct, separately-observable ways temporal reasoning breaks.

Failure 1 · Temporal boundary adherence

Answering about the wrong window

Question asked for the weight trend over the past year.

Base modelReported an increase "from 61.9 kg to 68 kg between 06/13/2021 and 12/18/2021" — a real trend, but drawn from more than two years before the asked-about window.
After tuningCompared 68 kg on 12/18/2021 against 64 kg on 01/21/2023 and concluded weight decreased over the past year.

The base model's numbers were real and correctly quoted. It simply answered about a different period than the one asked about — and reached the opposite conclusion as a result.

Failure 2 · Trend accuracy

Asserting a direction the record doesn't support

Question asked whether mixed hearing loss in the right ear had improved over time.

Base model"has shown some improvement over time"
After tuning"There is no indication of improvement"

Nothing in the record indicated improvement. This is a trend claim manufactured from a sequence of measurements that don't actually trend.

Failure 3 · Temporal precision

Attaching a measurement to the wrong time point

Question asked for growth progression from age 9 to 11 with specific heights.

Base model"At age 9, the patient's height was 135.3 cm (05/13/2011)" — that measurement was actually recorded at age 8.
After tuning"At age 9 (09/08/2011), the patient's height was 139 cm"

The value existed in the record and was quoted accurately — it was bound to the wrong point in time. This is the failure mode hardest to catch by reading an answer alone, because every individual fact in it is true.

How the benchmark was built

Model-generated, clinician-checked — with an honest asterisk

TIMER-Bench is synthetic by design: human curation of multi-year records doesn't scale, and the paper argues that's precisely why existing benchmarks are recency-biased. The generator is Gemini-1.5-Pro, prompted to produce pairs that carry dated evidence.

From the generation prompt (verbatim excerpts)

"Prioritize instructions that involve reasoning over the timeline of the patient's care (e.g., comparing test results over time, evaluating the progression or resolution of a condition, or assessing treatment impacts)."

"Refer to specific timestamps (dates only, in the format MM/DD/YYYY) to justify responses and highlight temporal reasoning."

"Avoid generating generic or irrelevant instructions that do not utilize the temporal nature of the EHR."

Questions were then filtered to keep only those with multiple time-stamped pieces of evidence — a structural guarantee that answering requires synthesis across parts of the record rather than a single lookup. This filter is what separates TIMER-Bench from the 71.3% of MedAlign that is retrieval.

Two caveats the paper states itself. First, the clinicians "were not provided with the patient-protected information to do a complete factual accuracy evaluation" — so that 98 means the responses read as contextually reasonable, not that they were verified against the underlying record. Second, the complexity scores vary sharply between raters: 77 / 63 / 99 across the three annotators. The 80 average conceals real disagreement about how hard these questions actually are.

A distinction worth drawing explicitly

"Longitudinal" means two different things across benchmarks

TIMER and CliniCARE-Bench both work over multi-year records, and both would describe themselves as testing longitudinal reasoning — but they are controlling for different things, and it changes what a good score means. The comparison below is this deep-read's own reading; neither paper frames itself against the other.

TIMER · coverage

Is the whole timeline being asked about?

Controls: where in the timeline questions point — via the normalized-position metric and deliberate re-sampling into recency / edge / uniform distributions.

Depth comes from: whatever the LLM generator happens to produce, then filtered structurally (≥2 timestamped evidence items) and spot-checked.

Catches: a benchmark that only ever asks about the last few months; a tuned model blind to the middle of a record.

CliniCARE-Bench · depth

Is the reasoning procedure specified well enough to score?

Controls: what a defensible answer requires — clinician-written adjudication criteria, required evidence lists, process rubrics, explicit precedence between verdict branches.

Depth comes from: the specification itself. Its renal-trajectory scenario mandates per-admission baselines, an unbiased measure, and separating a reversible episode from a trend.

Catches: a right answer reached by a shortcut; two annotators scoring the same case differently.

These are complementary, not competing. A structural filter guarantees an answer touches more than one point in time; it cannot guarantee the question needed real synthesis to answer. A clinician-authored specification guarantees depth for the scenarios it covers; it says nothing about whether those scenarios sample the timeline evenly. No paper in this corpus does both — a benchmark with CliniCARE's specification discipline and TIMER's distribution control does not yet appear to exist.

Reading the results with their scope attached

What this does and doesn't establish

The judge is validated on a narrow base

Correctness and completeness are LLM-judged. That judge was validated by rank-correlating its scores against clinician rankings on MedAlign — strong coefficients (ρ = −0.94 correctness, −0.89 completeness), but computed over a ranking of only five model systems, which is a thinner validation than the numbers suggest.

Gains are real but modest, and base-model dependent

The headline +7.3% / +9.2% comes from Llama-3.1-8B. On Qwen2.5-7B the same tuning moves MedAlign correctness only 58.42 → 60.40. The method generalizes in direction, not in magnitude.

Synthetic evaluation data, acknowledged as a tradeoff

TIMER-Bench is model-generated by design — the paper's argument is that this is the price of escaping recency bias, since human curation over decade-long records is what produced that bias in the first place. It is a defensible trade, not a free one.

One health system, one record format

Built on de-identified records from a single institution in OMOP-style structured form plus notes. The average TIMER-Bench span (1,294 days) is also notably shorter than MedAlign's (3,895 days), so it is longitudinal but not maximally so.
