Script Concordance Testing
Measuring whether a model changes its mind by the right amount

Full paper: "Assessment of Large Language Models in Clinical Reasoning: A Novel Benchmarking Study" · McCoy, Swamy, Sagar, Wang, Bacchi, Fong, Tan, Tan, Buckley, Brodeur, Celi, Manrai, Humbert, Rodman · NEJM AI 2025;2(10)

Most medical benchmarks ask whether a model knows the answer. This one borrows a decades-old medical-education instrument that asks something narrower and harder: given a hypothesis and one new piece of information, how much should that hypothesis shift — and is the model's shift the size an expert panel would make? Models that are explicitly optimized for reasoning turn out to do this worse, not better.

The instrument

What a script concordance item actually is

In plain termsA short case, then a prompt of the form: given diagnosis X as the working hypothesis, and now finding Y — does X become more or less likely, and by how much? The answer goes on a five-point scale from −2 (much less likely) to +2 (much more likely). The point isn't the diagnosis. It's the size and direction of the update.

SCT was designed for medical education to probe one specific skill: revising judgment as information arrives, in cases built to contain genuine ambiguity. Three item types appear in the benchmark — diagnosis, investigation, and treatment — differing only in what the hypothesis is:

Diagnosis SCT

"If you were thinking of [diagnosis]… and then you find [finding]… this diagnosis becomes:" — much less likely ↔ much more likely.

Investigation SCT

"If you were thinking of [test]… and then you find [finding]… this management becomes:" — much less appropriate ↔ much more appropriate.

Treatment SCT

"If you were thinking of [treatment]… and then you find [finding]… this management becomes:" — same appropriateness scale.

Why the scoring is unusual. There is no single right answer. Each item is scored against a reference panel of experienced clinicians: the modal expert response earns full credit, and options chosen by at least some experts earn partial credit proportional to how many experts picked them. Options no expert chose earn zero. The scoring is built to reflect that expert disagreement in ambiguous cases is legitimate — which makes it a very different scoring philosophy from the right/wrong benchmarks most models are tuned against.

The headline result

Reasoning-optimized models are spread across the whole range

Mean percentage of maximum possible score, averaged across four prompting conditions (zero-shot and five-shot, each with and without a request for explanatory reasoning). All models at default settings including temperature.

If explicit reasoning optimization reliably helped here, the red bars would cluster at the top. Instead o3 is first (67.8%) and Gemini 2.5 Pro Preview is last (52.1%) — both reasoning-optimized — while GPT-4o, a non-reasoning model, is second (63.9%). The paper notes o3's margin is "modest compared with its dominance on standard medical benchmarks," and that Gemini 2.5 notably underperforms its own predecessor, Gemini 1.5 Pro. Friedman tests confirmed the overall differences (P<0.001), with 22 significant pairwise differences on post hoc Conover testing.

A practical note on running the benchmark: parseable response rates ran 96.2–99.7% for most models, but DeepSeek R1 fell as low as 87% in one trial. Overall parseable rate across everything was 94.7%.

The human comparison

Above students, below senior clinicians — and the gradient itself is the validation

Where models land

Models matched or exceeded medical student performance on several of the ten tests. They consistently fell below senior residents and attending physicians — with a single exception, o3 on the Singapore Neurology SCT.

This is a sharp reversal of the usual pattern, where models beat humans on licensing-exam-style questions.

Why the gradient matters more than the gap

Across every test carrying multiple levels of human experience, scores rise monotonically with training level: students → junior residents → senior residents and attendings.

The paper treats this as face validity evidence — the instrument tracks clinical expertise in humans, so a model's position on it is interpretable rather than arbitrary. A benchmark where novices and experts score the same would say nothing about where a model sits.

A subtler comparison, easy to miss. Correlating question difficulty between models and students produced only weak correlations — especially for reasoning-focused models other than o3. Models often found easy the items students found hard, and vice versa. Student scores clustered near the top of the range while model scores spread more evenly, and every model's response distribution differed significantly from the students'. Matching a human score does not mean failing in human-like places.

The mechanism behind the gap

Models almost never say "this changes nothing"

The paper compares each model's distribution of chosen ratings against the distribution that would result from perfect concordance with the expert panels. Two systematic deviations appear.

Deviation 1 · universal

Every model underuses the neutral rating

All ten models chose 0 — "this new information does not change my hypothesis" — less often than the expert-concordant distribution requires. Mistral Small came closest to optimal.

Recognizing that a piece of information is not diagnostically relevant is itself a clinical skill. Models treat almost every new fact as evidence for or against.

Deviation 2 · concentrated in reasoning models

A pull toward the extremes

Reasoning-tuned models showed substantial bias toward −2 and +2 — the "much less / much more likely" ends. The paper singles out Gemini 2.5 and DeepSeek R1 as most affected, with o4-mini and o1-preview showing it to a lesser degree. o3's distribution was more balanced but still suboptimal.

The paper reads this as systematic overconfidence: exaggerated belief shifts where the case calls for a small probabilistic nudge.

The proposed explanation connects to a general finding outside medicine (Liu et al., 2024): chain-of-thought prompting systematically reduces performance on tasks where human verbal deliberation also hurts — implicit statistical learning, exception handling, subtle pattern recognition. Structured step-by-step reasoning may encourage premature commitment to a hypothesis, which is precisely the failure mode SCT is built to expose. The authors are careful to add that this "may be prompt sensitive" and should be read as a limitation under default prompting, not a ceiling on capability.

Why this isn't just another exam

SCT and multiple-choice measure different things — in humans too

The argument that SCT tests reasoning rather than recall doesn't rest on the model results. It rests on prior human evidence the paper cites:

No correlation in trained clinicians

In one study, emergency medicine residents showed no significant correlation between their SCT scores and their MCQ scores. The two formats rank the same people differently.

The correlation fades with expertise

Another found an MCQ–SCT correlation among medical students that disappears in later training years — as expertise deepens, SCT success depends less on encoded knowledge and more on flexible judgment.

This is the strongest available argument that a model's SCT score is not a restatement of its knowledge benchmark score. It also sets up the paper's interpretive claim: given robust evidence that models encode medical knowledge well, gains on SCT would plausibly indicate improved reasoning rather than more facts.

Reading it with its scope attached

Where concordance scoring strains

The superhuman paradox

Scoring rewards agreement with the expert distribution. A model that reasons better than the panel would be scored lower for departing from consensus. The authors name this directly as an epistemic limit of concordance-based metrics — and it is a general problem for any benchmark whose ground truth is expert agreement.

Two scoring artifacts in the format itself

Expert panels tend to avoid extreme responses, which can inflate scores for models that are simply conservative. And treating all expert opinions as equally valid risks awarding partial credit for answers that are objectively wrong.

The scale means different things to different raters

Some respondents read the extremes as markers of confidence, others as the size of a probability shift. That inconsistency clouds both human and model scoring, and it is unresolved.

Prompt sensitivity, and an abstracted task

Only standard prompting was tested — no retrieval augmentation, Bayesian framing, or tool use. And SCT deliberately strips context specificity: the individual, interpersonal, systems, and societal factors that shape real clinical reasoning. The authors note this limitation applies equally when SCT is used on humans.

Also worth noting for interpretation: aggregate scores mask variation across the four prompting conditions; the physiotherapy dataset introduces reasoning patterns not directly comparable with physician practice; and clinical reasoning is context specific, so expert consensus in one country may not transfer to another — the same geographic diversity that strengthens generalizability also complicates it.
