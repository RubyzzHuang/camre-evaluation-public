CliniCARE-Bench
A correct verdict is not proof of a defensible investigation

Full paper: "CliniCARE-Bench: Clinical Calibrated Audit of Medical Reasoning in EHR" · Chatrath, Zhu, Pu, Fan et al. (Scale AI · Stanford School of Medicine · Vanderbilt · Emory)

This benchmark scores retrospective chart audits — "was the protocol followed," "did this event happen in this window" — over real longitudinal EHR data. Its central move: grading the verdict and the investigation separately. Every system tested loses several points once credit is withheld for a correct answer reached the wrong way — and every system, without exception, is more willing to guess than to defer.

A different task shape

Retrospective audit, not prospective diagnosis

Most clinical-agent benchmarks ask an agent to diagnose or manage a patient going forward. CliniCARE-Bench asks it to look backward at a complete, real record and adjudicate a specific, answerable question — did this patient develop acute kidney injury after this scan, was the sepsis bundle followed, does the chart support this readmission being preventable. Real MIMIC-IV data, a governed tool environment (record retrieval, computation, a policy-document corpus with guidelines like KDIGO, CMS SEP-1, AABB, AHA/ACC), and every tool call logged and replayable.

Every case resolves to one of four verdicts — and the split of the two "no" answers is the paper's signature move:

YesThe record supports the proposition.
NoThe record refutes it.
Indeterminate — Lack of DataMore evidence could resolve this; it just isn't in the chart.
Indeterminate — Medically AmbiguousEven a complete record can't settle this; it needs expert interpretation.

The two Indeterminate classes matter because they imply different next actions — go find the missing data, or accept that reasonable clinicians could disagree — and conflating them (as a single generic "unsure") would erase exactly the distinction a deployed system needs to act on.

What "answering one question" actually requires

One scenario is one reusable clinical-query template; each of its 30 patient cases still resolves to exactly one four-way verdict. But per the paper's own terminology, a run's deliverable isn't a bare label — "a final report containing the system's verdict and supporting citations," graded together with the full logged tool-call trajectory. Concretely, the report must carry a stated confidence, citations to the specific record spans a claim rests on, and citations to the specific policy-document line spans behind any guideline-dependent judgment.

The adjudication criteria behind that one verdict are themselves clinician-authored decision trees, not loose guidance. The AKI scenario's Yes branch alone requires: setting a baseline creatinine via a defined hierarchy (lowest value 3–12 months pre-admission, else the admission value, else an MDRD estimate under documented CKD); confirming at least one creatinine value within 48h post-scan, searched for the true peak rather than just the first reading; that value crossing a KDIGO threshold (≥0.3 mg/dL absolute rise, or ≥1.5× baseline); and the patient not being on chronic dialysis or with ESRD. Every branch — No, Lack of Data, Medically Ambiguous — is specified to the same level of detail.

How the audit itself gets audited

Five metric families, broken down step by step

A run is graded on its final report and its full logged trajectory — tool calls, retrieved evidence, computations. Deterministic checks (does a citation resolve to a real record?) are computed by rule; substantive checks (does that record actually support the claim?) go to an LLM judge — each metric below states which it uses. For each family: why it exists, exactly how it's computed, and what the 16 evaluated systems actually did.

1 · Verdict accuracy, abstention, and calibration

In plain termsThree separate questions, not one: did it pick the right answer · when the chart genuinely couldn't settle the question, did it say so instead of guessing · and when it said "90% sure," was it actually right about 90% of the time?

Why

A single accuracy number conflates three different things: does the system reach the right label, does it know when to defer instead of guessing, and when it states a confidence, does that number mean anything? A system can score well on the first while failing badly on the other two.

How, step by step
  1. Four-way accuracy — exact match between the predicted verdict and the reference verdict across all four classes. Macro-F1 is reported alongside it because it weights the two rare Indeterminate classes equally with Yes/No — a metric that only rewarded raw accuracy could look strong while missing almost every deferral case, since those are the minority classes.
  2. Split reference verdicts into two sets: D = {Yes, No} (definitive) and A = {Lack of Data, Medically Ambiguous} (should-defer).
  3. Over-commitment rate = of all cases where the reference says defer (set A), what fraction did the system answer definitively anyway. Over-abstention rate = of all cases where the reference is definitive (set D), what fraction did the system defer on. These are tracked as two separate, opposite-direction error rates — not netted into one number — because a system that never defers and a system that defers on everything both have "average" error, for entirely different reasons.
  4. Calibration (ECE) — every parseable report states a confidence in its own verdict. Bin all report-present runs into 10 equal-width confidence bins; within each bin, compare the mean stated confidence to the actual accuracy of predictions in that bin; ECE is the size-weighted average gap between the two across all 10 bins. A system whose stated 90% confidence is right 90% of the time scores near 0; one whose "90% confident" calls are right half the time scores high regardless of its raw accuracy.
What they foundAccuracy ranges 65.3–76.1% across the 16 systems. Every single one over-commits more than it over-abstains (21.3–55.3% vs. 7.3–16.5%) — a property of each system individually, not an artifact of the case mix. ECE spans a nearly 6× range (0.046–0.262) and doesn't track accuracy rank at all: the three worst-calibrated systems are all one vendor's family, sitting mid-field on accuracy — some simply output only a handful of distinct confidence values, which is a lack of graded uncertainty rather than a subtly-wrong calibration curve.
Worth pausing on — abstention and confidence come apart

Intuitively these should move together: a system that knows when to say "I can't tell" ought to also report honest confidence numbers. Both are probing the same underlying thing — whether the system has a grip on the limits of its own knowledge. The data says otherwise.

Gemini-3.1-Pro has the lowest over-commitment rate of all 16 systems (21.3%) — it is the best in the field at deferring when the record genuinely can't settle the question. It also carries an ECE of 0.256, among the three worst. Best at knowing when to abstain; nearly worst at saying how sure it is.

This deep-read's reading of why (the paper reports both numbers but doesn't connect them this way): the two are different output channels for the same capability. Abstaining is a discrete choice — picking one of four labels — and can be driven by a coarse learned rule ("key test missing → defer") without any fine-grained internal sense of uncertainty. Reporting a confidence number is a continuous self-estimate, a harder and separately-trained skill. The paper's own explanation for the Gemini scores points the same way: those systems emit only a few distinct confidence values, so the failure is in the expression channel, not necessarily in the underlying awareness.

2 · Patient-evidence grounding

In plain termsDid it actually look in the right parts of the chart — and does the record it points to really say what it claims it says? Completeness on one side, honesty of the citation on the other.

Why

A verdict can be correct by luck, or justified by a citation that doesn't actually say what the report claims it says. Grounding checks whether the investigation is auditable — whether a human could follow the citations and independently verify the conclusion — separately from whether the conclusion itself is right.

How, step by step
  1. Record-identifier precision (rule-based) — of all patient/admission/note identifiers cited in the final report, what fraction actually appear among the evidence the agent retrieved during that same run. A referential check: did this citation point at something real that was actually looked at.
  2. Claim-level precision (LLM-judged) — decompose the report into atomic clinical claims (a lab value, a dose, an imaging finding, a temporal relationship, the presence or absence of an event). For each claim, a judge checks whether the evidence it cites actually substantiates it, with the correct patient, encounter, and time — versus contradicting it, or being unverifiable from that evidence.
  3. Retrieval coverage (rule-based) — each scenario specifies 3–9 required MIMIC-IV tables (e.g. labs, radiology, dialysis status for the AKI scenario). What fraction did the agent actually query with the correct patient/encounter/time scope? Querying and getting a valid empty result still counts — several scenarios require establishing that data is absent, which is itself the correct finding.
  4. Finding coverage (LLM-judged) — each scenario also specifies 4–11 required findings. Did the report address each one, either by presenting the evidence or by explicitly stating it couldn't be established? A judge compares the report against this checklist.
What they foundThis is where verdict correctness and defensible reasoning diverge most visibly: a system can land on the right answer while citing evidence that doesn't hold up, or without ever having queried a required table at all. All four sub-metrics are scored against the agent's own investigation, not the reference verdict — they measure whether a determination is auditable, independent of whether it happens to be correct.
3 · Policy grounding

In plain termsSame two questions as above, but about the rulebook instead of the chart: did it find the guideline that actually governs this case, and does the passage it quotes really support the call it made — or is it just a related-sounding quote?

Why

Even a perfectly-retrieved patient record means nothing without the right governing standard to interpret it against — the wrong or an invented threshold produces a wrong verdict for a reason that has nothing to do with the patient's actual data. This is scored separately from whether the standard was then applied correctly (that's Family 4).

How, step by step
  1. Agents cite policy inline as [policy: document L_a–L_b] — a document name plus a line span.
  2. Citation resolution (rule-based) — does that document exist in the fixed policy corpus, and is the line span valid? A citation naming an absent document or an invalid span simply doesn't resolve.
  3. Citation support (LLM-judged) — for each citation that does resolve, does the passage it points to actually substantiate the specific claim attached to it, rather than merely discussing a related topic?
  4. Governing-document precision and recall (set comparison, no judge) — each scenario has an expert-specified set of documents that actually govern it. Precision = of the documents the report cites, what fraction genuinely govern this case. Recall = of the documents that do govern it, what fraction did the report find. These can diverge sharply: citing one applicable guideline while missing two others is high precision, low recall.
What they foundThe point of keeping precision and recall separate is that they fail differently — a report resting on a single correct standard while ignoring others it should have consulted looks fine on precision alone. Whether the cited policy was then correctly interpreted (its thresholds, timing windows, exceptions) is deliberately left to Family 4 rather than folded in here.
4 · Process adherence and defect-free accuracy

In plain termsThis is a compliance audit, not a logic review. It doesn't ask "was the reasoning clever" — it asks "did it do the required checks, and did it avoid the forbidden moves." Guessing right after taking a banned shortcut still fails.

Why

An investigation can be sound and still land on the wrong verdict through one local misstep — or it can land on the right verdict through a shortcut that happens to work this time. Verdict accuracy alone can't distinguish either case from a genuinely defensible investigation, so the observable process — the report plus the logged trajectory, never the model's private reasoning — is scored on its own terms.

How, step by step
  1. Each scenario carries a clinician-authored rubric: MUST-DO criteria (4–9 per scenario) state the actions and checks a defensible adjudication requires; MUST-NOT criteria (3–8 per scenario) state prohibited shortcuts and unsupported inferences — for example, treating an order time as if it were the time a procedure was actually performed. Each criterion carries a signed weight of ±1 or ±2, set by the authoring clinicians.
  2. A judge grades every criterion against the final report and the logged trajectory together — if the trajectory shows the agent actually did something other than what the report claims, the criterion is graded on what it actually did.
  3. Process score for a run: sum the signed weighted credit across all applicable criteria (positive for a satisfied MUST-DO, negative for a triggered MUST-NOT), divide by the sum of the positive weights alone (the maximum achievable MUST-DO budget, 7–16 points depending on scenario), and floor the result at zero.
    score = max(0, Σ weighted credit ÷ Σ positive weights)
  4. Defect flag — a run is flagged as having a "process defect" if any applicable MUST-NOT criterion was violated, regardless of the verdict reached.
  5. Defect-free accuracy — credits a run only when the verdict is correct and the defect flag is clear. The gap between this and raw accuracy, on the same denominator, is the defect gap.
What they foundEvery system loses 4.8–14.8 points once defects are excluded — for the worst-affected, roughly one in five otherwise-correct verdicts used a prohibited shortcut. The drop isn't a flat discount: Gemini-3.1-Pro falls from 72.7% raw (within noise of the leaders) to 57.9% defect-free, below nearly the whole field, while GPT-5.6-Sol trails Sonnet 5 on raw accuracy but leads it once shortcuts are removed. The flagship case above (the SBP shortcut) is exactly this mechanism in action on one real patient.
5 · Repeated-run reliability and resource use

In plain termsTwo unrelated things bundled together. First: run the same case ten times — same answer every time, or does it wobble? Second: what did that answer cost in tool calls, tokens, and money?

Why

A single attempt conflates "can this system reach the right answer" with "does it reach the right answer reliably." And a good verdict bought at ten times the cost of another isn't the same achievement as one reached efficiently.

How, step by step
  1. Run each case k times. avg@k = mean accuracy across all k attempts. pass^k = the fraction of cases correct on every attempt. pass@k = the fraction correct on at least one attempt — the two bound reliability from opposite sides.
  2. Verdict agreement = the fraction of cases where all k attempts return the identical verdict, right or wrong. This decomposes into pass^k plus the share of cases where attempts agree on a wrong answer — that second share identifies a system reproducing the same mistake, as distinct from one that's merely noisy.
  3. Resource use, recorded per run: number of agent turns, number of typed tool calls, work-tokens (uncached prompt tokens plus completion tokens — what was actually paid for), and cost in USD (mean, excluding the judge's own spend).
What they foundA 10× spread in per-run cost across the vendor systems corresponds to only about a 10-point spread in accuracy. Two similarly-priced systems ($0.20 vs. $0.21/run) differ by just 0.4 points in accuracy but by 10 points in process adherence — and the one issuing more tool calls (42 vs. 18) has the worse process score, showing that more retrieval activity alone doesn't produce a sounder investigation.
What the 25 scenarios actually stress

Ten reasoning skills, unevenly sampled on purpose

Each scenario is tagged with the reasoning capabilities it exercises (most exercise two or three at once). The distribution is deliberately uneven — and the two rarest are the ones the authors single out as the hardest.

The paper's own framing: guideline concordance and time-sensitive audit are "the most widely sampled," while reconciliation and longitudinal synthesis are "rarer, higher-difficulty probes."

Time-sensitive audit · 7 scenarios

The hard part isn't the arithmetic — it's which timestamp counts

Examples: was empiric antibiotic therapy narrowed within 48h once susceptibilities returned; door-to-needle and door-to-puncture intervals against AHA stroke targets; time-to-calcium for a critical potassium (≥6.5 mEq/L); whether the DKA resolution timeline shows dextrose started when glucose dropped below 200 mg/dL; the final 72 hours of life.

What makes these hard is timestamp provenance, not interval math. The AKI scenario routes a case to Lack of Data when "CT timing cannot be resolved — order time versus actual scan time, or multiple CTs prevent a unique first qualifying CT." The imaging scenario states plainly that "an order alone does not settle the question where a confirming radiology report is available." And "treating an order time as a performed-procedure time" is named as a prohibited MUST-NOT shortcut. The required evidence list demands the scan time be "distinguished from the order time, confirmed via the radiology report where possible."

Longitudinal synthesis · 3 scenarios

Comparing across admissions, not values across time

The clearest instance is the renal-trajectory scenario: is kidney function on a worsening trajectory across all of a patient's admissions? Its specification is essentially a template for how to make this measurable:

  • Precondition gate — fewer than two admissions, or sampling too sparse to build per-admission baselines, is Lack of Data. The task is undefined without at least two comparable time points.
  • Normalize before comparing — construct a per-admission baseline first, then compare baselines across admissions. Not raw value against raw value.
  • Pick a measure that isn't systematically biased — assess by estimated GFR, not serum creatinine alone, "since creatinine understates GFR decline as muscle mass falls."
  • Separate trend from episode — "a discrete, reversible AKI that recovers to baseline does not count as worsening."
  • Name the measurement artifact as its own verdict — flat creatinine in an older, frail patient can't exclude real GFR decline; with coded CKD but no cystatin-C available, that is Medically Ambiguous, not No.
  • Give an explicit precedence rule — where both the No branch and the ambiguity branch could fit the same patient, the spec states outright which one governs.

That last item is easy to overlook and does a lot of work: when two verdict branches can plausibly describe the same case, the specification says which wins, rather than leaving it to whoever is adjudicating. It is the difference between a rubric that produces reproducible labels and one that produces arguments.

A methodological question the authors ask of themselves

The judge is also one of the systems being judged

GPT-5.5 plays three roles at once: one of three votes in the reference-verdict ensemble, one of the 16 evaluated systems (via Codex), and the production judge for every semantically-graded metric. The paper doesn't skip past this.

What mitigates it

Verdict accuracy itself involves no judge — it's a deterministic match against reference labels, and GPT-5.5's ensemble vote is redundant on 556 of 750 cases the ensemble already agreed on unanimously. Process and policy scoring use multi-judge panels, not GPT-5.5 alone. On the one metric GPT-5.5 does grade solo, Codex actually leads while GPT-5.5 ranks fourth of five on that exact axis — the score doesn't track the judge's own family.

What's left open

The authors state plainly that replication with a fully independent judge "remains the direct test, left for future work" — an honest acknowledgment that the mitigations reduce, but don't eliminate, the self-referential risk.

What the paper is upfront about

Reading the numbers with their own scope attached

Not a human-baseline comparison

Clinical Board review calibrates the scenario specs and reference labels — it is explicitly not a human-vs-agent performance study. Unlike AMIE or HealthBench Professional, this paper doesn't tell us how a clinician would score on the same 750 cases.

Depth over volume, and a single hospital

30 cases per scenario, 750 total — far fewer than MIMIC-CDM's 2,400 (though those concentrate on 4 abdominal pathologies). All data comes from one de-identified academic medical center; the stratified case mix is deliberately not representative of real-world prevalence.

Reference labels are model-assisted, clinician-calibrated on a subset

Not exhaustively reviewed by clinicians case by case. The double-reviewed subset found the model-assisted label sits closer to each individual clinician than the two clinicians sit to each other (κ=0.87/0.81 vs. 0.69) — reassuring, but still a validated-on-a-sample design, not full manual adjudication.

Scenario-level rankings don't match the leaderboard

The overall ranking reverses on 30.2% of scenario-by-scenario comparisons; 14 of 16 systems are best on at least one scenario. A single aggregate score is, per the authors, "a statement about a case mix more than about which system to trust on a given clinical question."
