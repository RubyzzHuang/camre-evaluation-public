CliniCARE-Bench
A correct verdict is not proof of a defensible investigation

Full paper: "CliniCARE-Bench: Clinical Calibrated Audit of Medical Reasoning in EHR" · Chatrath, Zhu, Pu, Fan et al. (Scale AI · Stanford School of Medicine · Vanderbilt · Emory)

This benchmark scores retrospective chart audits — "was the protocol followed," "did this event happen in this window" — over real longitudinal EHR data. Its central move: grading the verdict and the investigation separately. Every system tested loses several points once credit is withheld for a correct answer reached the wrong way — and every system, without exception, is more willing to guess than to defer.

A different task shape

Retrospective audit, not prospective diagnosis

Most clinical-agent benchmarks ask an agent to diagnose or manage a patient going forward. CliniCARE-Bench asks it to look backward at a complete, real record and adjudicate a specific, answerable question — did this patient develop acute kidney injury after this scan, was the sepsis bundle followed, does the chart support this readmission being preventable. Real MIMIC-IV data, a governed tool environment (record retrieval, computation, a policy-document corpus with guidelines like KDIGO, CMS SEP-1, AABB, AHA/ACC), and every tool call logged and replayable.

Every case resolves to one of four verdicts — and the split of the two "no" answers is the paper's signature move:

YesThe record supports the proposition.
NoThe record refutes it.
Indeterminate — Lack of DataMore evidence could resolve this; it just isn't in the chart.
Indeterminate — Medically AmbiguousEven a complete record can't settle this; it needs expert interpretation.

The two Indeterminate classes matter because they imply different next actions — go find the missing data, or accept that reasonable clinicians could disagree — and conflating them (as a single generic "unsure") would erase exactly the distinction a deployed system needs to act on.

How the audit itself gets audited

Five metric families, each scoring something different

A run is graded on its final report and its full logged trajectory — tool calls, retrieved evidence, computations. Deterministic checks (does a citation resolve to a real record?) are computed by rule; substantive checks (does that record actually support the claim?) go to an LLM judge. Both are used throughout, and each metric states which it is.

Verdict & abstention

Four-way accuracy + directional error

Macro-F1 weights the two rare Indeterminate classes equally with Yes/No. Over-commit = giving a definitive answer when the reference says defer; over-abstain = deferring when the reference is definitive — tracked as two separate rates, not one "calibration" number.

Patient-evidence grounding

Citation precision × evidence coverage

Precision: does a cited record actually exist and actually support the claim? Coverage: did the agent query the required tables and address the required findings — including cases where "confirming this data doesn't exist" is itself the correct finding.

Policy grounding

Does the cited guideline exist, and apply?

Citations reference a document and line span; resolution is checked by rule, whether the passage actually supports the claim is judged. Separately: precision (do the cited documents actually govern this case) and recall (did it find every document that does).

Process adherence

A weighted MUST-DO / MUST-NOT rubric

Each scenario carries clinician-authored criteria, ±1 or ±2 weighted. A violated MUST-NOT is a "process defect."

score = max(0, Σ weighted credit ÷ Σ positive weights)

Defect-free accuracy — the paper's central metric — credits a run only when the verdict is correct and no MUST-NOT criterion was violated. It is what separates "got the right answer" from "got the right answer defensibly."

Sixteen systems, four uncomfortable patterns

What raw accuracy alone would have hidden

The leaderboard reorders once shortcuts are penalized

Gemini-3.1-Pro scores 72.7% raw — within noise of the leaders — then falls to 57.9% defect-free, behind every Claude and Codex system but one. Meanwhile GPT-5.6-Sol trails Sonnet 5 by 0.7 points on raw accuracy but leads it by 1.9 points defect-free. The gap isn't a constant discount; it reshuffles who's actually ahead.

Every system over-commits more than it over-abstains

Over-commitment (giving a definitive answer when deferral was correct) ranges 21.3–55.3%; over-abstention ranges only 7.3–16.5%. The asymmetry holds for all 16 systems individually, not just on average — a property of how these systems behave, not an artifact of the case mix.

Calibration ranks the field completely differently than accuracy does

Expected calibration error spans a ~6× range (0.046–0.262) and doesn't track accuracy rank at all — the three worst-calibrated systems (all from one vendor family) sit mid-field on accuracy. Some of them simply emit only a handful of distinct confidence values — a lack of graded uncertainty, not a subtly miscalibrated curve.

Cost and tool-call volume don't buy defensibility

A 10× spread in per-run cost corresponds to only ~10 points of accuracy. Two similarly-priced systems ($0.20 vs $0.21/run) differ by only 0.4 points in accuracy but 10 points in process adherence — and the one making more tool calls (42 vs 18) has the worse process score.

Per-class performance makes the pattern concrete: Yes/No F1 stays strong across every system, while both Indeterminate classes collapse on the weaker ones — precision on the deferral classes runs as low as 26–67%. The failure is concentrated exactly where deferral matters most.

A methodological question the authors ask of themselves

The judge is also one of the systems being judged

GPT-5.5 plays three roles at once: one of three votes in the reference-verdict ensemble, one of the 16 evaluated systems (via Codex), and the production judge for every semantically-graded metric. The paper doesn't skip past this.

What mitigates it

Verdict accuracy itself involves no judge — it's a deterministic match against reference labels, and GPT-5.5's ensemble vote is redundant on 556 of 750 cases the ensemble already agreed on unanimously. Process and policy scoring use multi-judge panels, not GPT-5.5 alone. On the one metric GPT-5.5 does grade solo, Codex actually leads while GPT-5.5 ranks fourth of five on that exact axis — the score doesn't track the judge's own family.

What's left open

The authors state plainly that replication with a fully independent judge "remains the direct test, left for future work" — an honest acknowledgment that the mitigations reduce, but don't eliminate, the self-referential risk.

What the paper is upfront about

Reading the numbers with their own scope attached

Not a human-baseline comparison

Clinical Board review calibrates the scenario specs and reference labels — it is explicitly not a human-vs-agent performance study. Unlike AMIE or HealthBench Professional, this paper doesn't tell us how a clinician would score on the same 750 cases.

Depth over volume, and a single hospital

30 cases per scenario, 750 total — far fewer than MIMIC-CDM's 2,400 (though those concentrate on 4 abdominal pathologies). All data comes from one de-identified academic medical center; the stratified case mix is deliberately not representative of real-world prevalence.

Reference labels are model-assisted, clinician-calibrated on a subset

Not exhaustively reviewed by clinicians case by case. The double-reviewed subset found the model-assisted label sits closer to each individual clinician than the two clinicians sit to each other (κ=0.87/0.81 vs. 0.69) — reassuring, but still a validated-on-a-sample design, not full manual adjudication.

Scenario-level rankings don't match the leaderboard

The overall ranking reverses on 30.2% of scenario-by-scenario comparisons; 14 of 16 systems are best on at least one scenario. A single aggregate score is, per the authors, "a statement about a case mix more than about which system to trust on a given clinical question."
