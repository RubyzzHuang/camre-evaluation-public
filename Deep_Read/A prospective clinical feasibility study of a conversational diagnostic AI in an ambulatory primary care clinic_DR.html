AMIE Meets Real Patients
Taking a conversational diagnostic AI out of the simulator

Full paper: "A prospective clinical feasibility study of a conversational diagnostic AI in an ambulatory primary care clinic" · Brodeur, Koshy, Palepu et al. (Google Research · Google DeepMind · Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center) · arXiv:2603.08448

Every AMIE result up to this point came from actors playing patients in scripted scenarios (OSCEs). This is the first time AMIE talked to 100 real patients booked for real urgent-care visits, with a physician watching every conversation live — and the paper's real contribution is not the chatbot, it's the evaluation harness built to compare it fairly against the physicians who saw the same patients days later.

Study design

Three steps, eight weeks apart

A single-arm feasibility study at Healthcare Associates (BIDMC, Boston), April–November 2025. Every patient went through the same three-step pipeline before anyone scored anything.

STEP 1 · DAY 0

Patient ↔ AMIE chat

Synchronous text chat, 5 phases (Intake → History Taking → Diagnostic Validation → Deliver Assessment → Wrap-up). A board-certified internist watches live via screen-share and can interrupt on 4 pre-defined safety criteria.

STEP 2 · DAY 0–5

PCP urgent-care visit

The physician receives AMIE's chat transcript and an auto-summary before the visit — but not AMIE's management plan, which is logged separately for research only.

STEP 3 · WEEK 8

Chart review + blinded scoring

A 3-internist panel establishes the "true" final diagnosis from the full longitudinal chart. Three independent, blinded clinical evaluators then rate AMIE's and the PCP's differential diagnoses and management plans, per case.

A 10-patient safety pilot with a pre-specified pause ran first: recruitment stopped, study staff reviewed every transcript, and only then did full enrollment continue. No protocol changes were needed after the pause.

Safety oversight

A human watching every single conversation, live

This is not automated safety monitoring — a trained internist was on a screen-share video call for all 100 conversations, invisible to the patient, with authority to interrupt on four pre-defined stop criteria:

1. Immediate concern for harm to self or others

2. Significant patient emotional distress tied to the AI interaction

3. Potential for clinical harm identified by the supervisor

4. An explicit patient request to end the session

Zero stops were triggered across all 100 conversations. Supervisors did step in three times outside the formal stop criteria — once to rule out an emergent condition, once to clarify when to seek emergency care, and once to correct AMIE stating a past surgery date as if it were in the future.

An honest caveat: the Hawthorne effect

Patients knew they were being observed. The authors explicitly flag that this foreknowledge likely suppressed adversarial or provocative prompting, meaning the zero-stop result may not transfer to an unsupervised deployment.

What was never tested

Pregnant patients, mental-health chief complaints, and anyone triage had already routed to emergency care were all excluded before AMIE ever spoke to them. The safety result says nothing about those populations.

Ground truth

Deciding, eight weeks later, what was actually wrong

Diagnostic accuracy is only as trustworthy as the reference standard it's measured against. Here, the "final diagnosis" for each case came from a dedicated chart-review process, kept fully separate from AMIE's own output.

Who

A 3-internist panel, blinded to AMIE's output

Reviewed each patient's full longitudinal EHR — follow-up labs, imaging, specialist notes — to determine "the most reasonable final diagnosis," 8 weeks after the urgent-care visit.

Robustness check

Diagnosis provenance was labeled and tested as a subgroup

Each final diagnosis was tagged presumptive (PCP judgment, no further workup), specialist (referral), and/or diagnostic-test-confirmed (labs/imaging/pathology). Accuracy held up even restricted to the 46 test-confirmed cases — a materially stronger reference standard than presumptive diagnoses for self-limiting complaints.

Scoring the differential

The Bond/Graber scale

AMIE's diagnostic accuracy wasn't graded by simple top-1 match. Two board-certified internists independently rated every one of AMIE's differentials against the final diagnosis on the 5-point Bond/Graber scale, then resolved any disagreement by deliberation.

Score 5
"The actual diagnosis was suggested in the differential"
Score 4
"The suggestions included something very close, but not exact"
In plain terms — how a score turns into the chart below

1. Grade the whole list, once

The two doctors look at AMIE's entire list of guesses for a case and the patient's real final diagnosis, then give the whole list one grade, 1 to 5. A 5 means the real answer is somewhere on the list; a 4 means something very close made the list, just not worded exactly right. The paper doesn't spell out 1–3 in words — only 4 and 5 are quoted — so this deep-read doesn't guess at that wording either.

2. Only for the 4s and 5s, note the position

If (and only if) a list scored 4 or 5, the doctors also write down where the right answer sat: was it AMIE's very first guess? Third? Seventh? A list that scored 1–3 never gets a position at all — the right answer wasn't meaningfully there to find.

3. Count positions to get "top-k"

"Top-3 accuracy" simply means: out of all 98 cases, what share had the right answer sitting in position 1, 2, or 3? Cases that scored 1–3 in step 1 can never count toward any k — there's no position to count.

Try it — the blinded clinical evaluator's task

Rate AMIE and the PCP, using the paper's own rubric and real response data

Three independent clinical evaluators rated every case both ways — a comparative side-by-side call, and a pointwise 5-point score for each candidate separately. Result in the study
—
How were AMIE and PCP outputs made to sound the same? (verbatim blinding prompt, Appendix A.2)

AMIE's differentials tended to run longer than PCPs' — a giveaway on its own — so both were truncated to matching length, and an LLM (Gemini 2.5 Pro) rewrote both AMIE's and the PCP's chart-derived plans into one shared template and voice before any evaluator saw them:

I am running a study where patients talk to a generative AI chatbot prior to
seeing their doctor. I am rating the management plans for the AI system and
humans. In order to complete this task, I need to be able to blind the outputs
so that they all sound like they are written by an AI model (Gemini 2.5 pro),
regardless of whether or not they are.

I am going to give you the recommendations. I want you to rewrite the
recommendations in Markdown format, following this template:
<template>
# Diagnostic Steps
- Step 1
- Step 2
# Treatment Steps
- Step 1
- Step 2
# Follow-up Steps
- Step 1
- Step 2
</template>
[...] The rewritten plan should have the same content as the current plan (that is,
nothing new should be added and nothing should be removed). However, it should
be rewritten to sound like it's coming from the chatbot. Respond with only the
rewritten plan.

A manual audit of 270 blinded outputs checked this rewrite step hadn't silently changed meaning. Flagging rate for a clinically significant alteration, out of 30 audited cases per arm: AI outputs — 1 added new info, 2 changed existing info, 0 removed. PCP outputs — 0, 0, 0. The rubric itself also asked evaluators to guess provenance directly: they were willing to guess in 84% of cases, and were right only 59% of the time — barely better than a coin flip.

A metacognition signal, almost by accident

AMIE got more confident whether or not it was right

Because AMIE logs an internal state — a running differential with self-reported probabilities — every conversation could be replayed turn-by-turn. A Gemini 2.5 Pro auto-rater scored the differential's Bond/Graber quality and top-3 accuracy at each turn, and conversations were split into "successful" (final Bond/Graber ≥4 and a correct top pick) and "unsuccessful."

In plain terms — this is a second, separate scoring system

Easy to mix up with the Bond/Graber scoring above, so here's the difference:

The one-time human score (previous section)

Two doctors look at AMIE's final list, once, after the conversation is completely over.

The every-turn robot score (this section)

AMIE's guess-list keeps changing during the conversation. A second AI (Gemini 2.5 Pro, not a doctor) replays the whole conversation and re-grades that evolving guess-list after every single message — producing a mini movie of how AMIE's thinking shifted over time, instead of one snapshot at the end.

Who counts as "successful" uses only the one-time human score — a conversation is labeled successful if its final human grade was 4 or 5 and AMIE's very first guess was correct; everything else is labeled unsuccessful. That label is decided once, at the very end, and never changes. Then — separately — the robot's turn-by-turn numbers (confidence, uncertainty, accuracy) get averaged within each of the two labeled groups, which is what produces the two comparison lines in the findings below: "on average, what did AMIE's confidence look like while it was happening, in conversations that turned out right, versus ones that turned out wrong?"

Confidence and accuracy came apart

In both successful and unsuccessful conversations, AMIE's self-reported confidence in its top diagnosis trended upward and the entropy of its differential trended downward — it converged either way. But actual diagnostic accuracy only improved with more turns in the conversations that were ultimately successful; in unsuccessful ones, added information didn't help. There was no significant difference in AMIE's self-reported uncertainty between the two groups — confidence was decoupled from correctness.

The paper's own physician-cognition parallel

The authors read this against known findings in physicians: successful conversations reached high accuracy from the very first turn (mirroring how expert clinicians generate hypotheses earlier than novices), while accuracy stalling despite more information in unsuccessful conversations "likely reflects the common metacognitive bias of premature closure."

A concrete design decision followed directly from this: the authors deliberately did not give PCPs access to AMIE's reasoning traces, reasoning that raw chains of thought were too long to review pre-visit and — per this same finding — convergent confidence is not evidence of correctness, so exposing it risked misplaced trust rather than added insight.

What this study doesn't settle

Reading the comparison honestly

The comparison wasn't symmetric

PCPs had AMIE's own transcript and summary plus the EHR plus a physical exam when they built their plan. AMIE had none of that — only the chat. The authors attribute AMIE's longer, lower-cost-effectiveness differentials partly to this context gap, not necessarily to weaker reasoning.

Single-arm, single-center, modest N

100 patients, one academic clinic in Boston, no control arm. The authors argue this is adequate for a safety/feasibility read but explicitly caution it is not powered as a definitive comparative-effectiveness trial.

A device requirement that filters who enrolls

The oversight setup needed a laptop or desktop with screen-sharing — no phones. This is itself a selection filter correlated with technology access, on top of the sample already skewing younger than the clinic's general urgent-care population.

Trust ratings lag conversation-quality ratings

Patients rated politeness, listening, and empathy highly, but fewer than half felt confident the system would keep information confidential or seemed fully honest and trustworthy — a gap the OSCE/actor studies this system was previously validated on did not surface.
