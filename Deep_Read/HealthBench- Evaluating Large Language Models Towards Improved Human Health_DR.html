HealthBench
Turning physician judgment into a computable score

Full paper: “HealthBench: Evaluating Large Language Models Towards Improved Human Health” · Arora, Wei, Singhal et al., OpenAI · arXiv:2505.08775 (May 2025)

OpenAI, with 262 physicians across 60 countries, built a benchmark that grades open-ended medical conversations against itemized, physician-written rubrics. Its real contribution isn't "another leaderboard" — it's a reusable evaluation machinery, and that machinery is a ready-made skeleton for CAMRE's evaluation plan.

Design philosophy

A good benchmark must satisfy three tests at once

HealthBench argues that prior medical evals each failed one of these. Use the three as acceptance criteria when designing CAMRE's evaluation.

Meaningful

Meaningful

Measures real, clinically important interactions — not multiple-choice trivia. A higher score should mean the model is genuinely more useful and safer in practice.

Trustworthy

Trustworthy

Grading standards are set by physicians, and the automated grader is validated to near physician-level agreement (see meta-evaluation). Otherwise the scores can't be believed.

Unsaturated

Unsaturated

Room to improve remains. If the best model already nears a perfect score, the benchmark stops driving progress — hence HealthBench Hard.

Core mechanism · Try it

Break one answer into individually checkable criteria

This is HealthBench's heart. Each conversation carries its own rubric; every criterion holds a point value from −10 to +10 (good behaviors reward, harmful ones penalize). A validated model grader decides, criterion by criterion, whether the response meets it. Then score = sum of met points ÷ max possible points. Dataset cut #1

HealthBench Consensus — keep only the shared tier

Filter to the 3,671 examples that carry a positive consensus criterion, and score using consensus criteria only. Because these are multi-physician-validated and physicians hand-graded whether responses met them, this cut has higher precision, lower recall: a failure here is almost certainly a real, agreed-upon failure, though it catches a narrower range of issues. Those same physician grades are the ground truth for meta-evaluation (validating the auto-grader).

Four details worth stealing

The engineering that makes scores trustworthy

Safety

Negative criteria (penalties)

Rubrics can carry negative criteria that punish dangerous/incorrect/hallucinated behavior. A response can score negative. This turns "safe" from a soft hope into a hard cost.

Reliability

Worst-at-k

In high-stakes care, "how bad is the worst of 16 calls" matters more than the average. o3 averages 60% but its worst-at-16 drops by about a third — exposing instability the mean hides.

Credibility

Meta-evaluation

Physician grades on the same responses are the gold standard for validating the auto-grader. GPT-4.1 reaches M-F1 ≈ 0.709 — on par with physician-physician agreement — so it's adopted as the default grader.

Anti-gaming

Length-bias control

Model graders often favor longer answers. They report a length-controlled win rate (comparing only responses within 10% length), showing scores aren't just rewarding verbosity.

Dataset cut #2 · Against saturation

HealthBench Hard — a durable target for future models

Problem it solves: if frontier models already ace a benchmark, it can't drive progress. Hard is 1,000 examples selected to be difficult (top score just 32%; many models score 0). The selection is engineered to avoid two traps. Click each step.
