Full paper: “Medical Reasoning in LLMs: An In-Depth Analysis of DeepSeek R1” · Moëll, Sand Aronsson, Akbar (KTH · Karolinska · Region Stockholm) · April 2025

Unlike the benchmark papers, this is an audit: a clinician reads DeepSeek R1's actual reasoning on 100 MedQA cases and asks how it thinks, not just whether it's right (it's right 93% of the time). The two findings that matter for us: a taxonomy of how the reasoning fails, and a surprising, cheap signal for when to distrust it.

What this paper is

An audit of the reasoning, by a clinician

No new benchmark or model — instead, evaluate how a reasoning model arrives at its answer. The method is a simple, repeatable reasoning-audit protocol.

STEP 1

Ground-truth check

Compare the model's final answer to the MedQA reference to find the error cases.

STEP 2

Decompose the reasoning chain

Split the chain-of-thought into discrete decision points (identify → rule out → compare options → decide), map each to a hypothetico-deductive step (data → problem representation → differential → treatment choice), and tag errors with a cognitive-bias category.

STEP 3

Expert validation

A practising clinician reads every error case and judges where and why the reasoning deviated from medical-reasoning best practice.

How rigorously did the paper actually do this? Loosely — the "taxonomy" is not a published codebook, and Step 3 was a single clinician reading 7 error cases, with no second rater and no inter-rater agreement reported. Borrow the shape, not the rigor.

Try it — the flagship finding

Reasoning length as a "reverse certainty" signal

The most useful result: wrong answers had far longer reasoning (mean 8,118 chars) than right ones (mean 3,648; Welch t = −2.74, p = 0.032). Verbose reasoning may signal uncertainty — or the model rationalizing a wrong conclusion. Drag the threshold: flag any output longer than it as "low-confidence" and watch how well length alone catches errors.

Balance

What it got right — and a caveat to keep

Sound reasoning

Real hypothetico-deductive structure

In the 93% it got right, the reasoning was genuinely clinical: gather data → form a problem representation → build a differential → eliminate/compare options → decide (e.g. choosing nitrofurantoin for cystitis in pregnancy by ruling out contraindicated/broad/resistant options).

Caveat

Mind the anthropomorphization

The authors flag it themselves: labelling an LLM's mistakes "anchoring bias" is inferred from the reasoning text and is somewhat speculative. Useful for human understanding, but we shouldn't assume the model has human-like cognition. Also: n = 100, only 7 errors, MCQ format, one model.
