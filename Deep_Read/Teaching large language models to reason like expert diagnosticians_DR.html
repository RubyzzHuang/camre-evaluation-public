CPC-Bench & Dr. CaBot
Evaluating the process, not just the diagnosis

Full paper: “Teaching large language models to reason like expert diagnosticians” · Buckley, Conci, … Rodman, Manrai (Harvard Medical School · Beth Israel Deaconess) · 2025 · cpcbench.com

For a century, AI has been tested on the NEJM Clinicopathologic Conferences — but only on the final diagnosis. This paper argues the real work of an expert is the reasoning: arguing a nuanced differential, grounding each step in the literature, integrating conflicting data, navigating uncertainty. It builds a benchmark (CPC-Bench) and an agent (Dr. CaBot) to evaluate exactly that.

The core argument

A final diagnosis is the tip; the reasoning is the iceberg

Prior CPC evaluations scored only whether the model named the right disease. But a CPC's value is the process an expert demonstrates to peers. This paper reorients evaluation toward that process.

The old way

Final-diagnosis accuracy only

Score the model on whether the right disease appears in its answer. Misses everything that makes diagnosis hard: weighing competing hypotheses, citing evidence, updating as new information arrives, handling missing data.

This paper

Evaluate the reasoning itself

Argue a complete differential with supporting/refuting evidence, ground each claim in the literature, update the diagnosis as the case unfolds, and stay robust when key findings are withheld. CPC-Bench scores all of these.

Three more designs worth stealing

Scoring reasoning, plans, and missing data

Reasoning

Evidence triples

The Clinical Reasoning task scores (diagnosis, evidence, confirm/disconfirm) triples with precision & recall against the expert's discussion. It grades whether the model gives the right supporting and refuting evidence per hypothesis — reasoning, not just the answer.

Plans · supports P1

Testing-plan 0/1/2 rubric

2 = matches or is a superset of the actual plan · 1 = different but would still have helped reach the diagnosis · 0 = wouldn't have helped. The "1" tier explicitly credits valid-but-different plans.

Robustness

Information Omission

Remove the explicitly-normal findings — the negatives a clinician must actively seek ("no lymphadenopathy", "CT unremarkable") — and measure the accuracy drop (~2–3%). Tests reliance on actively-gathered context.

Is the evaluation trustworthy?

Two layers of validation

Blinded pairwise (Turing-style)

Physicians couldn't tell AI from human

Across 62 paired differentials (CaBot vs the original NEJM discussant), blinded physicians identified the source correctly only 26% of the time — i.e. misclassified 74%. They then scored each on a Likert scale for four dimensions: overall differential quality, justification of each item, citation quality/accuracy, and learner engagement — rating CaBot consistently favourably.

Physician-validated LLM judge

The automatic scorer tracks physicians

A GPT-4.1 judge grades the free-text tasks. Validated against 1,467 physician-annotated differentials: 86% accuracy, F1 89%. For the Testing-Plan rubric, 84% accuracy / weighted F1 83% against physician scores. Only then is the judge trusted at scale.
