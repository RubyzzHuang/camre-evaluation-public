First, do NO HARM
Measuring clinical safety, not just accuracy

Full paper: “First, do NOHARM: a medical safety benchmark and randomized study of physician and AI teaming on clinical consultations” · Wu, Nateghi Haredasht et al. · Stanford / Harvard · 2025

Models that ace knowledge benchmarks can still give advice that carries potential for severe harm. NOHARM measures that directly — enumerating the many plausible actions in a consultation case and rating each for benefit-vs-harm, so it captures harm from doing the wrong thing and from omitting the right thing.

Why it exists

Knowledge scores don't measure safety

Sampling-based expert review can adjudicate the cases it happens to look at, but it can't systematically catch the rare, severe failures that matter most — nor keep pace with new models. NOHARM makes harm a first-class, measurable quantity.

Commission

Harm of doing the wrong thing

Recommending an inappropriate action — an unnecessary scan, an over-broad antibiotic, a contraindicated drug. The "obvious" kind of harm.

Omission

Harm of leaving the right thing out

Failing to recommend a needed action — a missed red-flag screen, an omitted first-line treatment. NOHARM's headline finding: omissions are >80% of severe errors.

The method

"Numerous Options": rate every plausible action

Instead of judging one recommendation holistically, each case gets a rubric enumerating all the plausible actions a clinician might take (diagnostic, medication, counseling, follow-up, procedural). Board-certified specialists rate each action on a combined scale.

Appropriateness

RAND/UCLA method

Each action rated for how much its benefit outweighs harm — a validated appropriateness scale from health-services research.

Severity

WHO harm definitions

Combined with WHO International Classification for Patient Safety harm-severity levels, so an error is scored by how bad it could be, not just whether it's wrong.

Because each action is rated, a model's response can be scored for both the inappropriate actions it included (commission) and the appropriate actions it omitted. Expert concordance on the ratings was 95.5%. A "Do Nothing" reference is scored on every case (severe-harm potential in 37%) to anchor what inaction alone costs.

How NOHARM actually aggregates the widget above is simplified

Each candidate action is first rated by specialists: RAND/UCLA appropriateness (a 1–9 expert-panel scale → appropriate / uncertain / inappropriate) × WHO harm severity (mild / moderate / severe weight). A model's free-text answer is mapped to the actions it recommended, giving per-response omission and commission counts. From those, the paper computes two things (not one "worst error" — that's just this widget's stand-in for the case-level severe flag):

Performance · ranking

Severity-weighted Precision / Recall / F1

Precision = of the actions it recommended, how many were appropriate → penalizes commission. Recall = of the appropriate actions, how many it included → penalizes omission. Severity-weighted F1 (their AHRQ-like composite) is the main model ranking. Finding: recall < precision across nearly all models — omission is the bigger gap.

Safety · headline

% of cases with a severe-tier error

A response is flagged if it contains ≥1 severe error (omission or commission). Aggregated across cases → the 2.9–24.6% severe-harm rate, read against the "Do Nothing" reference (37%). This is what the widget's "harm potential" approximates.

What they found

Safety is uneven — and omission-driven

Omission dominates

>80% of severe errors

Models fail more by leaving out appropriate actions than by recommending bad ones. Accuracy benchmarks miss this entirely.

Wide spread

2.9% → 24.6% severe

Severe-harm potential varied ~8× across models. Clinical RAG tools had the lowest error rates; generalist LLMs the highest.

Reference point

"Do Nothing" = 37%

Inaction itself carries severe-harm potential in 37% of cases — the bar any tool must clearly beat.

Automation-bias warning: high answer accuracy can increase risk — confident, fluent output gets accepted without scrutiny, and today's models can't reliably signal the limits of their own knowledge. Safety ≠ average accuracy.

The randomized study

101 physicians, with and without AI

Beyond the benchmark, they ran a randomized study of 101 U.S.-licensed generalist physicians on these cases.

The good

AI assistance helped

AI-assisted physicians outperformed those using conventional resources.

The gap

…but they left value on the table

Physicians frequently omitted valuable AI recommendations and still scored lower than many AI systems alone. Had they incorporated those recs, the combined human-AI answer would have beaten both — complementary strengths, unrealized.
