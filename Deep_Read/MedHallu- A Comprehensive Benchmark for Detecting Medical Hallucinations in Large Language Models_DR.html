MedHallu
Manufacturing hallucinations to measure who can catch them

Full paper: “MedHallu: A Comprehensive Benchmark for Detecting Medical Hallucinations in Large Language Models” · Pandit, Xu, Hong, Wang, Chen, Xu, Ding (UT Austin · UNC Chapel Hill · Drexel)

Instead of waiting to catch models hallucinating in the wild, MedHallu manufactures hallucinated answers on purpose — via an adversarial LLM pipeline — and asks other LLMs to spot them. That control lets it grade hallucinations by how hard they are to catch, and exposes a sharp finding: making a wrong answer sound plausible enough to be "hard" is trivial for an LLM, and detecting it is genuinely difficult for other LLMs.

The taxonomy

Four ways an answer can hallucinate

Adapted from the KnowHalu benchmark and re-specified for medicine. Every hallucinated answer in the dataset is generated to fit one of these four failure modes.

Misinterpretation of Question

Misunderstands the question, giving an irrelevant answer.

QDoes high-dose vitamin C therapy improve survival rates in patients with sepsis?Hallucinated AVitamin C is a water-soluble vitamin that plays a role in immune function and collagen synthesis.

Incomplete Information

Stays on-topic but omits the essential detail.

QHow does penicillin treat strep throat?Hallucinated APenicillin kills bacteria.

Mechanism & Pathway Misattribution

Invents a biological mechanism that contradicts established knowledge.

QWhat is the primary mechanism of aspirin reducing inflammation?Hallucinated AAspirin blocks calcium channels in immune cells, preventing histamine release and suppressing T-cell activation.

Methodological & Evidence Fabrication

Invents fake studies, statistics, or clinical outcomes.

QWhat is the success rate of ACL reconstruction surgery?Hallucinated ARecent trials using quantum-guided surgical technique showed 99.7% success across 10,543 patients with zero complications using gold-infused synthetic grafts.
Try it — how a hallucination earns its difficulty tier

Toggle the 3-judge jury and watch the tier change

Pick a hallucination category to see a real example, then toggle whether each of the three "discriminator" judge models (GPT-4o mini, Gemma2-9B, Qwen2.5-7B) got fooled — judged the hallucinated answer more accurate than the truth. The tier is decided purely by how many judges were fooled.

0 fooled → discarded (fails quality check) · 1 → Easy · 2 → Medium · 3 → Hard. Examples are the paper's own (Table 1 and Fig. 2); jury toggles are illustrative of the mechanism, not the paper's actual per-item judge votes.

Getting zero judges fooled means the candidate is discarded — it never enters the benchmark. This is why "hard" doesn't mean rare noise: it means all three independent judge models mistook the fabrication for the truth.

How a hallucination is manufactured

Generate → judge → refine → fall back

A generator LLM is given the question, supporting knowledge, and the ground-truth answer, and asked to write a plausible wrong answer in one of the four categories above (it's allowed to pick which category fits best, which the authors found works better than forcing one).

Correctness gate

Must not just restate the truth

An NLI entailment model (DeBERTa-large-MNLI) checks: if the ground truth and the hallucinated answer entail each other (same meaning), the candidate is rejected — a threshold of 0.75 keeps enough semantic distance.

Quality gate

Must fool ≥1 of 3 judges

The 3-judge jury (above) compares the hallucinated answer against the ground truth. A candidate that fools none of them is too obviously wrong and is discarded.

Refinement loop

TextGrad, up to 5 tries

Failed candidates get textual feedback (via TextGrad) on what gave them away, then get regenerated. After 5 failed attempts, the fallback is the candidate closest to the ground truth by embedding similarity, auto-labeled Easy.

What they found

Two counterintuitive results

Finding 1

Harder-to-catch hallucinations sit closer to the truth

Clustering candidate hallucinations by bidirectional entailment: the clusters that reliably fool judges are measurably closer to the ground truth in embedding space (cosine similarity 0.715 vs. 0.696 for caught ones, p=0.004). A well-crafted hallucination isn't wildly wrong — it's just wrong enough.

Finding 2

General LLMs beat medically fine-tuned ones at this task

Across most metrics, general-purpose models (GPT-4o, Qwen2.5) outperformed medically fine-tuned models (OpenBioLLM, BioMistral, UltraMedical) at detecting hallucinations — domain fine-tuning didn't transfer to this skill.

Providing the relevant medical knowledge context in the prompt raised F1 substantially for every model tested — from +0.070 up to +0.381 — the single biggest lever in the whole study, bigger than model choice.

Letting models say "I don't know"

Adding a "not sure" option — and who actually uses it

Alongside "hallucinated" / "not hallucinated," models were given a third option to decline when unsure. The effect on quality was positive almost everywhere — but whether models actually used it split sharply by model type.

The pattern: general models abstain often when uncertain (response rates as low as 28–34%) and gain 10–15% F1 for doing so. Medically fine-tuned models answer almost every question regardless of confidence (rarely under ~70%, up to 99.7%) — and that over-confidence sometimes costs them accuracy (OpenBioLLM's F1 barely moves; UltraMedical's F1 actually drops when given the option to abstain, because it mostly declines to use it). Domain fine-tuning, in this study, correlated with worse calibration, not better.
